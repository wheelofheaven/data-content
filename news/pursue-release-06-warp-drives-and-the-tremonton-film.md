## What was released

On September 18, 2026 — six weeks after Release 05 — the United States Department of War posted the sixth tranche of declassified records under the **[Presidential Unsealing and Reporting System for UAP Encounters (PURSUE)](/wiki/pursue/)** at war.gov/UFO. By the Department's count as reported at publication, the release contains **71 files** — 55 documents, 15 videos, and one audio recording — 67 of them from the Department itself and four from a **local law-enforcement agency in Colorado**, the first contributor in the program's history that is not a federal body. The documents run from 1952 to 2025, and 64 of the 71 carry redactions.

That local contribution is the tranche's structural novelty, and it is small: four cellphone videos and a transcript of a police officer narrating what he was filming. Everything else in Release 06 is Department of War material, and most of it belongs to a single documentary chain — the contract paper and the reference library of the **Advanced Aerospace Weapon System Applications Program (AAWSAP)**, the Defense Intelligence Agency's 2008–2012 study of "breakthrough" aerospace threats, whose commissioned papers on warp drives and wormholes made the headlines.

The Department's accompanying statement repeats the formula of the fifth word for word, with one numeral changed. Chief Pentagon spokesman Sean Parnell announced the sixth release, confirmed the rolling cadence, and added: "The Department of War and our agency partners are actively working on the next release of UAP files." No file-count breakdown was published; the counts above are the Department's as relayed by CBS News, and the portal's own index — as captured later the same day — lists 72 entries, the difference being the officer's transcript.

The notable items include:

- **The AAWSAP paper trail, 2008–2010.** The Defense Intelligence Agency's *Statement of Objectives* of July 18, 2008 defines the program as a contractor-supported effort to understand "the physics and engineering" of possible breakthrough aerospace technologies "out to the far term, i.e., from now through the year 2050," with the focus explicitly "not on extrapolations of current aerospace technology." It lists twelve study areas: lift, propulsion, control, power generation, spatial/temporal translation, materials, configuration and structure, signature reduction, human interface, human effects, armament, and supporting topics. The solicitation and original order of September 22, 2008 awards the work to **Bigelow Aerospace Advanced Space Studies, Inc. (BAASS)** — the Las Vegas firm of the aerospace entrepreneur Robert Bigelow — under contract HHM402-08-C-0072, and five modifications between September 2009 and September 2010 exercise the first option year, fund it at a reduced level "with impacts to deliverables," change the contracting officer's representative, and finally extend performance to December 21, 2010 without additional money. Every page is redacted somewhere.

- **Thirty-seven Defense Intelligence Reference Documents.** The release includes 37 of the 38 reference papers the program produced between December 2009 and January 2011 — the index calls them "reference and synthesis products rather than original research" and prefaces each with a warning that its summary "should not be read as implying current validation of the concepts discussed." The exotic titles are the ones the press led with: *Warp Drive, Dark Energy, and the Manipulation of Extra Dimensions* (April 2010), *Traversable Wormholes, Stargates, and Negative Energy* (April 2010), *Antigravity for Aerospace Applications* (March 2010), *Negative Mass Propulsion* (January 2011), *Advanced Space Propulsion Based on Vacuum (Spacetime Metric) Engineering*, *Concepts for Extracting Energy from the Quantum Vacuum*, *Invisibility Cloaking Theory and Experiments*, and *Cockpits in the Era of Breakthrough Flight*, which asks what a flight deck would need if a craft could control gravity and inertia. The larger part of the shelf is conventional survey work — metallic glasses, biomaterials, high-power microwaves, high-energy lasers, ultracapacitors, metamaterials, hypersonic tracking, brain-machine interfaces, aneutronic fusion, laser-launched nanosatellites, quantum and DNA computing, and the cognitive limits on one operator flying several spacecraft. Two papers stand apart. *An Introduction to the Statistical Drake Equation* (March 2010) recasts the familiar estimate of the number of communicating civilizations in the galaxy as a probability distribution — a DIA threat program formally modeling how many other civilizations there are, and how far away. And *Maverick Inventor Versus Corporate Inventor* concludes that the next breakthroughs are likeliest to come from independent, lightly institutionalized researchers — a profile, the index's own annotator observes, that "closely resembles the kind of researcher who appears to have authored or shaped much of the broader AAWSAP DIRD effort," a "notable methodological circularity."

- **The injury paper.** *Anomalous Acute and Subacute Field Effects on Human Biological Tissues* (March 2010) surveys reported injuries "allegedly associated with close exposure to anomalous field effects" and tries to match them to the known physiology of intense radiofrequency and microwave exposure — heating, burns, neurological and sensory disturbance. CBS News, which read the 38-page study, reports that it describes three antenna engineers who suffered "heat and redness on their skin and hair loss," one of whom showed "signs of radiation illness" and later "developed signs of malignant transformations." The index's annotator is blunt about the method: the findings "rely heavily on case comparisons lacking physical examinations, untestable exposure conditions, and a mix of established science and more controversial literature," extended to "anomalous-craft scenarios" that "lack independent scientific validation in the report."

- **Tremonton, Utah, July 2, 1952 — the Newhouse film and its file.** The archive's floor for moving images drops to the summer of 1952. Delbert C. Newhouse, a U.S. Navy Chief Warrant Officer and Photographer's Mate with more than a thousand hours of aerial photography, was traveling with his family near Tremonton when he saw, by his report, twelve to fourteen bright reflective objects moving in loose formation, and exposed about thirty feet of 16mm Kodachrome on a handheld Bell & Howell Auto Master. He handed the film to the Air Force. Release 06 publishes the film — a National Archives digitization of a 16mm print — together with its complete **Project Blue Book** case file: the investigators' assessment of Newhouse as "honest and reliable" and an expert witness; the successive hypotheses of sunlight on birds, on balloons, and a mirage; a December 1952 session at which Blue Book staff, balloon specialists, and representatives of General Mills, then the only maker of "pillow balloons," agreed the objects "closely resembled pillow balloons in flight" — with one General Mills man dissenting that they "appeared somewhat too bright and seemed to maneuver too rapidly"; the failure to find any balloon release in the area; and the later Air Force assessments that settled, more confidently, on seabirds reflecting sunlight. The file records what the film cannot give: with no fixed reference point in frame, "the objects' distance, size, altitude, or linear speed" could not be reliably determined. Alongside sit the Blue Book photo file with General Mills's sample balloon imagery, Newhouse's final personnel record (he left the Navy in 1957 as a Chief Warrant Officer 4), and the Air Force Director of Intelligence's "Flying Discs" file for September–December 1952, whose pages 26–36 concern the case. The film's later life is well known in the historical literature — the Navy's photographic interpreters spent months on it and declined the bird explanation before the CIA-convened Robertson Panel of January 1953 preferred the gulls — and Release 05's 1953 film analysis (DOW-UAP-D098) is cross-indexed as part of the set.

- **Ruppelt in Boston, March 1952.** A transcript and a roughly hour-long audio recording preserve a presentation by Captain Edward J. Ruppelt, the Air Force officer who ran the newly reorganized investigation that became Project Blue Book that same month. Ruppelt tells his audience that of some 800 historical cases reviewed, about twenty percent "remained unexplained after technical analysis," sourced from "credible military personnel, commercial pilots, and other qualified technical observers"; that reports concentrated "around sensitive national security and nuclear weapons installations (including Los Alamos, Albuquerque, and Oak Ridge)"; that radar had registered "unconfirmed air speeds between 1,000 and 3,500 miles per hour"; and that the Air Force had "no conclusive evidence regarding the nature or origin of the phenomena." He previews the instruments Blue Book would deploy — diffraction-grating cameras, radar-scope synchronization — and argues for "a systematic physical data collection effort." In CBS's rendering of the tape: "We've gone through these reports and come up with the fact that about 20% are very good reports offered by very reliable people and … just absolutely no conclusions can be drawn."

- **Colorado, October 2023 — the police officer's phone.** The local law-enforcement debut consists of four videos shot on the rear camera of a commercial cellphone — 1 minute 19 seconds, 15 minutes, 27 seconds, and 1 minute 42 seconds — and an edited transcript of the officer's narration. The officer described the phenomenon as "silent" and as featuring "red, blue, and green flashing lights." The All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office blurred the lower half of each frame to protect "the privacy or other protected interest of a U.S. person or commercial entity," and attaches to every clip the same caution: cellphones "employ autofocus and other automated image-processing functions that may distort the apparent morphological and kinematic characteristics of distant subjects, particularly at high magnification."

- **Iraq, 2022; the Middle East, 2025; the Yellow Sea and East China Sea, 2023.** The rest is the program's familiar sensor material. Two Central Command mission reports from Iraq in 2022 describe objects "flying sporadically within the target area … at various speeds between 80 and 180 miles per hour," one to two meters in size, altitude undetermined, with infrared stills whose visible trails the index attributes to "known image-compression and processing effects." Two Middle East reports from 2025 describe "a white round orb … moving as fast as 500 miles per hour" and "six small spherical objects grouped together," agile and "frequently changing direction," at an estimated 480 miles per hour, with a five-minute infrared video attached to the first. Six Indo-Pacific Command clips — five over the Yellow Sea and one over the East China Sea in 2023, between nineteen seconds and a minute and a half — show a sensor tracking "an area of contrast" and say nothing more. Every item is catalogued as unresolved.

## What this is, in plain terms

**Source claim.** Release 06 is the first tranche whose center of gravity is neither a sighting nor a sensor but a *program*: the government's own contract with a private firm to survey the physics that a far-term aerospace threat might exploit, published with its statement of work, its award, its funding history, and 37 of its 38 deliverables. The contributing roster reaches, for the first time, below the federal government. The cadence has stretched: after four intervals of two, three, four, and four weeks, the fifth ran six. The portal's searchable database now holds 447 files across the six releases.

**Comparative observation.** The AAWSAP shelf is the documentary substrate of the modern American UAP story. The program is the one the *New York Times* revealed in December 2017 under the name of its successor effort, and most of the reference papers themselves reached the public through Freedom of Information Act productions in the years since. What Release 06 adds is not the papers but their provenance: an official, indexed, consolidated record of who commissioned what, when, and for how much, under the Department's own annotations. Those annotations are the tranche's most striking feature. The index treats *Antigravity for Aerospace Applications* as work that "deviates significantly from mainstream physics consensus," the warp-drive paper as a "speculative chain" that "offers no viable engineering pathway," and the negative-mass paper as resting on "premises that remain unestablished in consensus physics." The archive is publishing the program and, in the same breath, declining to endorse it.

Three cautions attach to the headline material. First, the reference documents are surveys, and the Department says so; nothing in them reports a craft, a retrieval, or a measurement of anything anomalous. Second, the Tremonton case is the archive's oldest film and, on the Air Force's own eventual reading, its likeliest identification — birds — published with a print so degraded that its last fifteen seconds show a demonstration of sewing equipment. Third, the numbers in this dispatch are the Department's as relayed by the press and the portal's index as captured on the day; the two differ by one file, and the index's own dates are inconsistent in places noted below.

## Where this sits in the Wheel of Heaven framework

The [Age of Aquarius](/timeline/age-of-aquarius/) chapter treats the sequence of official UAP engagements as the first of the age's Signs of Acceleration (§IX). The first five dispatches read Releases 01–05 as the first five datable points on the disclosure curve the corpus places in the 2026–2030 window. Release 06 is the sixth — and it is the point at which the archive turns from what the government *saw* to what the government *studied*.

**Wheel of Heaven interpretation, marked as such.** The canon's account of the [Elohim](/wiki/elohim/) states, as a matter of record rather than of physics, that they came to this planet from another and left again: interstellar travel is, within the source material, an accomplished fact of engineering. The AAWSAP shelf approaches the same subject from the opposite side. It is not a report of anyone's arrival; it is a government's inventory of what any such arrival would require — lift, propulsion, "spatial/temporal translation," signature reduction, and the effects of such systems on human bodies — commissioned as a threat assessment with a horizon of 2050. That the Defense Intelligence Agency paid, in the same program, for a statistical treatment of the Drake equation — a formal model of how many communicating civilizations exist and how far apart they are — is the kind of convergence of subject the corpus records without weighing. The framework's own answer to the Drake question rests on the canon's reports, not on a probability distribution; the papers establish nothing about anyone's craft, and the Department's annotations say as much. Registered, not enlisted.

The Ruppelt recording carries a second thread. Release 04 documented the "nuclear nexus" — the 1949 green fireballs over Los Alamos, the 2015 Pantex report — and the corpus reads the atomic threshold of 1945 as the hinge of the present age. In March 1952, the first head of Project Blue Book was already telling an audience that the reports clustered around Los Alamos, Albuquerque, and Oak Ridge. The pattern the corpus identifies in the archive was visible to the archive's own first custodian, seventy-four years ago, in the month his program was born.

The registration stops there, because the corpus's [reception posture](/wiki/ufology/) requires it to. Under that posture — registration without enlistment — no unresolved case in this or any PURSUE release is evidence for the framework, and no case the record resolves is evidence against it. The Tremonton film is the posture's illustration: a witness the Air Force rated honest and expert, a film it studied for years, and an identification — seabirds — that the Air Force itself came to prefer. An archive that publishes its probable identifications alongside its unknowns is behaving like an archive, and the corpus registers that, as it did for the Bahia file in Release 05, in the program's favor. The [skeptical layer](/wiki/military-cover-up-of-exobiology/) is working in public view, and this time it is annotating the government's own research program.

## Where this sits in the broader trajectory

**Wheel of Heaven interpretation, marked as such.** The more consequential event of the interval was not the release but what preceded it by four days. On **September 14, 2026**, the Department of War issued what it calls a "targeted legal waiver": current and former service members, civilian personnel, *and contractors* who hold or once held UAP-related national defense information are authorized to disclose it to designated PURSUE representatives, and the waiver "supersedes civil and administrative enforcement provisions" of the nondisclosure and special-access-program agreements they signed — "strictly for communications directed to the PURSUE team." The Department's own statement names the barriers it means to remove: "concerns regarding potential legal liability, loss of clearances, or administrative prosecution." The waiver declassifies nothing and authorizes no public disclosure; whatever comes in goes through "systematic review, security evaluation, and potential declassification." The Pentagon declined, when asked, to say who the PURSUE representatives are.

That closes, on the Department's side, the edge the [program's hub entry](/wiki/pursue/) flagged as its most closely watched: ODNI's July 31 guidance had left the question of *contractors* — the population at the center of the 2023 congressional testimony — to follow-on guidance. The Department has now answered for its own agreements. Michael Gold, president of Redwire Space and a former NASA official who supported the Navy's UAP Task Force, called the action "nearly, if not entirely, unprecedented." The corpus reads it in structural terms: six tranches have opened the archive of what the government *has*; the waiver is the infrastructure for learning what the government's people *know*. The claims of 2023 — retrievals, remains, reverse engineering — are exactly the kind of thing that would have to arrive through this channel, and the corpus holds both available readings open: that no such material exists to be disclosed, which is AARO's stated finding, or that it exists and has now, for the first time, a protected road into the record.

**Open question.** Whether anyone walks that road, and whether what they bring survives review into a future release, is the durability test the framework actually cares about. The cadence has lengthened to six weeks, and Parnell's statement says the next release is actively in work. The corpus will track Release 07 when it appears.

## Source tensions and unresolved issues

1. **The unresolved-case framing remains in force — and the Department's annotations are its strongest yet.** No Release 06 item is characterized as confirmed non-conventional, and the corpus does not so characterize any. The reference documents carry explicit disclaimers against "current validation," and several are annotated as departing from mainstream physics. The corpus treats the annotations as part of the record.

2. **The counts differ by one.** The Department's figures as relayed at publication give 71 files (67 Department of War, four local law enforcement; 55 documents, 15 videos, one audio); the portal's index as captured later on September 18 lists 72, with 56 documents and five local law-enforcement items — the addition being the transcript of the officer's narration. This dispatch attributes both and asserts neither.

3. **Thirty-seven of thirty-eight.** The index's boilerplate describes each reference document as "one of 38" produced under the program; 37 are in the release, and press tallies say 37. Which paper is absent, and why, the release does not say.

4. **The index's dates are inconsistent in places.** The 2025 Middle East video is described in its own entry as footage "in 2024"; the fourth Colorado clip is titled October 2023 but described as filmed in January 2024; and the entry for the 2022 Middle East mission report reproduces the six-spheres description belonging to the 2025 report. This dispatch follows the titles for dates and flags the discrepancies.

5. **The papers are not new; the provenance is.** Most of the AAWSAP reference documents previously reached the public through Freedom of Information Act productions. Release 06's contribution is the official contract chain and the Department's annotations — a consolidation, not a revelation.

6. **The Tremonton print is a composite.** The index's own comment records conflicting references to 35mm and 16mm formats, a print whose last fifteen seconds show unrelated sewing-equipment footage, and the loss of the Idaho scenery Newhouse shot on the same roll. The film's evidentiary limits — no fixed reference point, no determinable distance or speed — were the Air Force's conclusion in the 1950s and remain so.

7. **The waiver's scope is the Department's own.** It supersedes agreements "executed within the United States" and reaches the Department's service members, civilians, and contractors; the intelligence community's contractor question remains with ODNI's follow-on guidance, and the PURSUE representatives to whom disclosures are to be made are unnamed.

8. **The cadence is now six instances, and it has lengthened.** Four intervals held at or under four weeks; the fifth ran six. A single longer interval is a datum, not a trend, and the multi-year durability the framework treats as the load-bearing test remains untested. The next data point is Release 07.
