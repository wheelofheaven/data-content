*The World Behind the Odyssey* ended in a trench. Beneath Christopher
Nolan's film lay the poem; beneath the poem, an oral tradition; beneath the
tradition, a collapsed Bronze Age world; and beneath that world, an eastern
inheritance of divine successions, serpent combats, assemblies, and stolen
fire that was already old when Greek was first written down. The excavation
found no bottom — it found a direction. The strata tilt east. This second
half of the pair follows them: out of the Aegean, along the trade routes,
into Mesopotamia and the Levant, to ask what the tilt actually means.

The question has a respectable mainstream answer, and this article's first
duty is to present it at full strength, because everything the corpus wants
to say afterwards stands on it. The mainstream answer is that archaic Greece
absorbed an enormous quantity of Near Eastern story, image, and technique,
along routes that archaeology can trace ship by ship. The corpus's further
question begins only where that answer stops: whether the shared stories
have, beneath their documented journeys, a shared referent.

## Two proems

The Oxford philologist M. L. West, in the chapter of *The East Face of
Helicon* (1997) devoted to the *Odyssey*, sets the two epics' openings side
by side. The Greek poem begins with a man defined by distance and knowledge:

> Tell me, Muse, of the man so versatile, who wandered
> far and wide after he had sacked Troy's holy citadel.
> Many were the peoples whose cities he saw and whose mind he came to know,
> and many the woes he suffered in his heart at sea
> as he ventured his life and his comrades' safe return. (*Odyssey* 1.1–5)

The Babylonian epic — in its Standard version, compiled centuries before
Homer{{ footnote(id="1") }} — begins with another such man:

> [Of him who] saw everything let [me te]ll the land,
> [of him who] knew [the entire]ly [let me tea]ch his whole story. …
> [He was compl]eted in wisdom, he who kn[ew] the entirety;
> he saw the [se]cret, and opened up what was concealed;
> he brought back intelligence from before the Flood.{{ footnote(id="2") }}

A far-traveller who saw the cities and minds of many; a far-traveller who
saw everything, opened what was concealed, and carried knowledge back from
beyond an unbridgeable distance. West notes that several scholars have been
struck by the similarity, and his own verdict on the wider pattern is
measured and firm: the wanderings strand of the *Odyssey* — the sea of
marvels, the woman on her island, the journey to the world of the dead, the
knowledge that costs — shows "an especially strong and clear relationship
with the Gilgamesh epic."

The precision of that sentence matters as much as its strength. West is
equally explicit about what the relationship is *not*: Odysseus, the
phlegmatic and calculating survivor, is a hero of an entirely different
temperament from the glorious, grieving king of Uruk. "He is, then, no
Gilgamesh." What the Greek poem inherited was situational — scenes,
settings, narrative shapes into which its own, differently imagined hero
was placed. The tradition borrowed the journey. It did not borrow the man.

That distinction — shapes travel, identities do not — is the methodological
key to this entire article, and it was hard-won. The companion piece traced
the same pattern in the theomachies: the succession myth travelled from
Hurrian Anatolia to Hesiod's Boeotia down to its grotesque particulars,
while each tradition bent the plot to its own obsessions. The *Odyssey*'s
eastern debt behaves identically. Scholarship can show the material moving.
What the material *is* — invention, inheritance, or memory — is a separate
question, and the poem itself does not answer it.

## The corridor

How did Babylonian narrative reach an Aegean singer? Not mysteriously. The
eighth and seventh centuries BCE — precisely the era in which the Homeric
poems crystallised — are the best-documented period of east–west contact in
the entire ancient record before Alexander.

The Swiss philologist Walter Burkert, in *The Orientalizing Revolution*
(1992), assembled the human side of the case: itinerant Near Eastern
craftsmen, seers, and healers working inside Greek communities, carrying
their practical religion and their stories with them. Robin Lane
Fox's *Travelling Heroes* (2008) mapped the geographic side: Euboean
traders settled at Al Mina on the Syrian coast, sailing home beneath the
very mountain — Hazzi to the Hittites, Kasios to the Greeks — on whose
slopes the storm god had fought his serpent in both traditions. Mary R.
Bachvarova's *From Hittite to Homer* (2016) reconstructed an older,
Anatolian channel: festival epic moving through the bilingual milieus of
Late Bronze Age and Iron Age Asia Minor into Aegean song. And the alphabet
in which the *Odyssey* was eventually fixed is itself the exhibit nobody
disputes — Phoenician letters, adapted by Greeks who were doing business
with Levantine partners close enough to borrow a writing system from.

The Greeks themselves did not regard their debts as shameful, or even as
particularly hidden. Herodotus, writing in the fifth century, reports as
common knowledge that the names of nearly all the gods came to Greece from
outside — from Egypt, in his account (*Histories* 2.50). His geography of
the debt differs from the modern one; his instinct that archaic Greece was
a *receiving* culture, junior to the older civilisations east and south of
it, is exactly what the tablets and trade routes have since confirmed.
Bruce Louden's *Homer's Odyssey and the Near East* (2011) catalogues the
result at book length. West states the cumulative case in the sentence that
unsettled his discipline, quoted already in the companion piece: Greek
literature is a Near Eastern literature.

## What borrowing proves

Here the argument reaches the edge that this article exists to mark,
because a tempting inference lies one step ahead, and the corpus declines
to take it in the dark.

The inference runs: if Homer borrowed from Gilgamesh, then Greece is
downstream of Mesopotamia; and if the fountainhead of Western literature is
downstream, then the Near East is the origin of civilisation itself — the
biblical theatre, the land of {% wiki(slug="tower-of-babel") %}Babel{% end %}
and {% wiki(slug="sodom-and-gomorrah") %}the cities of the plain{% end %},
holds seniority over the classical world.

Half of that conclusion is true, and none of it follows from the borrowing.

It is true on independent grounds. Cities, writing, monumental
architecture, law, astronomy, and literature appear in Mesopotamia
millennia before anything comparable appears in the Aegean: Uruk was a
metropolis with a scribal bureaucracy in the fourth millennium BCE, more
than two thousand years before any Greek literature was written down. Archaeology established that priority, stratum by
stratum, and no one contests it.

The literary borrowing, though, proves something smaller and more precise:
contact, and prestige. Archaic Greeks absorbed material from cultures they
recognised as older, richer, and more learned than their own. Borrowing
shows the direction of cultural gravity in the eighth century; it does not
by itself show where civilisation began, any more than medieval Europe's
absorption of Arabic science makes Baghdad the origin of Europe. Sliding
from "the stories came east to west" to "civilisation came east to west"
treats a philological fact as an archaeological one. The archaeological
fact happens to point the same way — which is exactly why the discipline of
keeping the two arguments separate costs the corpus nothing and buys it
honesty. The Near East's seniority rests on excavated ground, and would
survive even if every Homeric parallel dissolved tomorrow.

What the borrowing adds is intimacy. It shows that Greece was inside the
Near Eastern cultural sphere — reading its images, singing its plots,
worshipping recognisable cousins of its gods — during the very centuries in
which Greek religious tradition took the shape the West inherited. Greece
was a young province of an old world, and its poets knew it.

## Two engagements

Everything to this point is mainstream scholarship, stated with its own
confidence levels. What follows is the corpus's reading, and its epistemic
status is `framework`: explicit in the {% wiki(slug="raelism") %}Raëlian{% end %}
canon, developed by the corpus, and endorsed by no mainstream
discipline.{{ footnote(id="3") }}

The canon's account of Greece has two anchors, set centuries apart, and the
distance between them is the argument.

The first anchor is familiar from the companion piece.
{% libref(book="the-book-which-tells-the-truth", chapter=5, verse=54) %}TBWTT 5:54{% end %}
lists the regions where the creators — the
{% wiki(slug="elohim") %}Elohim{% end %} — maintained bases, and Greece is
named alongside the Andes and the Himalayas, with a characterisation
attached: a land "where Mythology also contains great testimonies." On the
canon's account, Greek myth is testimony in the same sense the biblical
corpus is testimony: a human record, transformed by transmission, of the
same creators and the same events. The
{% wiki(slug="theomachy") %}Theomachy{% end %} entry and the companion
article develop what those testimonies are read as remembering; the point
here is *when* this layer must refer to. Whatever the Greek tradition
testifies about was already embedded in it when Hesiod systematised the
succession of heaven — the testimony layer is archaic or older, in place
before the classical era began.

The second anchor is nothing like the first. In its reading of the book of
Daniel, the canon reports a discrete, dated, and frankly political
intervention:

{% library(book="the-book-which-tells-the-truth", chapter=3, verse=214) %}
If the Jewish people was dominated by the Persians and by the Greeks, it is because the creators, to punish it for its lack of faith, placed men of theirs, «angels», among these peoples, in order to have them accomplish technical progress explaining the great moments of their civilizations.
{% end %}

The passage goes on to name the head of the Persian delegation: Michael,
whom Daniel describes exactly where the canon points —

{% library(book="daniel", chapter=10, verse=13) %}
But the prince of the kingdom of Persia withstood me twenty-one days; but, behold, Michael, one of the chief princes, came to help me because I remained there with the kings of Persia.
{% end %}

— and Daniel 10:20 announces the "prince of Greece" as next, which is where
the canon's Greek emissaries stand.{{ footnote(id="4") }} Providence is
inverted: the empires that crushed Israel were rivals the creators had
*equipped*, as punishment. The emissaries are technology transfer with a
delegation head and a grievance.

Now set the two anchors on a timeline, because the chronology does real
work. Persian domination of Judea begins in 539 BCE; the Greek kingdoms
take over after Alexander, in the fourth century. The Homeric and Hesiodic
poems crystallised in the eighth and seventh centuries — roughly two
hundred years before the first emissary in this account could have set
foot among the Greeks. The conclusion is one the corpus binds itself to:
**the emissaries explain nothing in Homer.** Whoever taught Greece in the
classical era, the *Odyssey* and the *Theogony* were already old when the
lesson began. The documented archaic transmission — the poets, the traders,
the craftsmen of the corridor — and the canonical classical engagement are
two different transfers, by two different mechanisms, in two different
centuries, and any reading that blurs them refutes itself on the canon's
own dates.

## The sphere

Kept distinct, the two anchors support something larger than either: a
continuity. Greece appears in the canon at the deep layer — a base region
whose mythology preserves testimonies — and appears again in the classical
layer, receiving emissaries during the era of the empires. Engagement at
two moments, centuries apart, different in kind each time. The corpus reads
this as the minimum outline of a continuous fact: **Greece lay within the
Elohimian sphere throughout** — the same field of activity whose centre of
gravity the biblical corpus records in the Near East, from Babel to the
prophets.

Read this way, the mainstream picture and the canonical picture snap into
registration. Scholarship finds Greece culturally embedded in the Near
East, absorbing its stories through documented human channels. The canon
finds Greece *historically* embedded in the same theatre — a province of
the one engagement, holding its own testimonies rather than copies of
anyone else's. The embedding that philology documents is what the sphere
looks like from the outside; the testimonies are what it preserved on the
inside. Greek mythology is, on this account, a broader extension of the
biblical record: the same events, remembered west of the theatre's centre,
in a different language and a different pantheon, with the irreducible
differences that honest comparison preserves.

Two boundaries keep the reading disciplined, and both are recorded in the
corpus's claim records rather than merely promised here. First, the sphere
proposes no transmission mechanism. The routes by which the succession myth
or the Gilgamesh material reached Greece are philology's property, the
human corridor described above, and the corpus claims nothing about them —
the engagement layer concerns what the travelling stories were *about*, one
level beneath the question of how they moved. Second, the priority this
article began with is reframed, and narrowed. Seniority of the Near
East is a statement about where the engagement was concentrated first — the
biblical theatre, in the canon's account, and the cradle of cities in
archaeology's — and about nothing else. It is a priority of engagement and
flourishing. It ranks no peoples and no languages, and the corpus's records
exclude the ranking explicitly.

## The strongest case against

The counter-case gets its own section, stated at full strength, as the
house practice requires.

First and sharpest: **transmission suffices.** Every parallel this article
has cited — proems, wanderings, succession myths, combat plots — is fully
explained by the documented corridor. Stories moved because people moved.
A reading that adds a shared historical referent beneath the shared stories
currently predicts nothing that the boring explanation does not, and the
corpus's own claim record says so in the same terms: until the sphere
reading predicts a textual particular that transmission alone would not,
it earns the label `framework` and nothing stronger.

Second, the **thin-canon objection**. Two brief passages — one clause in an
enumeration, one paragraph on Daniel — are being asked to carry a
continuity spanning a millennium and a half. The canon does not narrate
what happened in Greece between the testimony era and the emissary era, and
the corpus's reading declines to invent it; still, a critic is entitled to
say that "continuously within the sphere" is a great deal of thesis for two
anchors, and the corpus records that objection as the claim's first
revision trigger.

Third, the **Daniel problem**. The text the canon reads for the emissaries
reached its final form, by mainstream dating, in the second century BCE —
after the Persian era it narrates. A passage composed
in retrospect testifies differently from a contemporary record, and the
canon's reading does not currently engage the point.

Fourth, the standing audit: **category substitution**. Translating divine
patronage of empires into technology-transfer delegations re-describes
Daniel's angelology in the prestige idiom of a technological age — so runs
the objection that scholars of religion have pressed against Raëlian
exegesis generally, and it applies to this article's material with full
force. The corpus's answer is the same discipline it applies everywhere:
the reading must eventually earn its keep in predictions, or be
surrendered.

## The trench continues east

The companion piece ended by calling Homer a doorway rather than a floor.
This article has walked through the doorway and measured what lies behind
it: a corridor of ships and singers that scholarship has mapped, an old
world whose seniority archaeology established independently, and — on the
canon's reading — a single long engagement in which Greece was a province
from the beginning, testifying in its own voice, taught again near the end.

The pair of articles now forms one argument. The *Odyssey* assumed a world;
that world assumed an inheritance; the inheritance came from the east; and
the east, in the corpus's reading, is where the makers worked first and
longest. Each step in that chain carries a different kind of confidence,
from mainstream consensus to framework commitment, and the corpus's records
keep the grades separate precisely so that a reader can accept the
philology while refusing the frame — or follow both, and know at every
step which one is bearing the weight.

The trench does not end at Babylon either. Beneath the Standard version of
Gilgamesh lie Sumerian poems a thousand years older; beneath the flood
tablet, the [corpus's own translation](/library/gilgamesh-woh/) of which
sits in the library, lies the Atrahasis tradition this
corpus has long worked through. The excavation continues where the records point —
east, and down.
