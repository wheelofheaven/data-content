A Portuguese man o' war is not an animal. It is thousands of them.

What drifts on the surface as a single blue creature — one gas float, one
train of stinging tentacles trailing beneath, one apparent will — is a
colony of separate bodies called zooids, all budded from a single
fertilized egg, each a distinct individual, and each so specialized that
it can no longer do anything but its one job.{{ footnote(id="1") }} One
zooid is the float and nothing else. Others are only mouths; others only
stingers; others only the machinery of reproduction. Pull them apart and
each one dies, because none of them was ever built to live alone.
Biologists argue, in good faith and without resolution, over whether to
call the man o' war a colony or an organism — a *they* or an *it* — and the
honest answer is that the question quietly stopped having an answer
somewhere in its evolution, at the point where the parts surrendered the
power to survive on their own. Past that point the distinction between many
and one is not false. It is simply no longer the interesting fact about the
thing.

This Explainer is about the possibility that humanity is drifting toward
that same point — and about what to call the science that would study it if
it is. A companion piece,
[*The Shape of Humanity*](/articles/the-shape-of-humanity/), argued that a
whole discipline is implied by a single sentence of the Raëlian canon, in
which {% wiki(slug="yahweh") %}Yahweh{% end %} asks {% wiki(slug="rael") %}Raël{% end %}
to consider «each human to be like a cell of the great being in gestation,
that is, Humanity».{{ footnote(id="2") }} That article proposed a name for
the discipline — *civilizational morphology* — and, before settling on it,
floated two others and set them side by side. The plainest was
*civilizational biology*. The most symmetrical was **macrobiology**:
microbiology studies the cell and the units of its scale, so a macrobiology
would study the systems *composed of* biological units the way a cell is
composed of microbiological ones.{{ footnote(id="3") }} And then that
article did something scrupulous. It kept *morphology* in front, and gave its reason:

> A morphology claims only to read the shape of the whole; a *biology*
> asserts that the whole is itself alive. The second is the stronger claim,
> and a discipline ought to earn it rather than smuggle it in through its
> own name.

That caution was correct, and this Explainer accepts it in full. It also
takes it as a dare. The reason to prefer *macrobiology* is not that it
sounds better. It is that the stronger claim is the one the canon actually
makes — flatly, without hedging — and that biology, in the century since it
learned how one living thing becomes many-into-one, has built exactly the
tools needed to test whether the claim can be earned. A discipline should
be named for what its object is doing. If the object is merely shaped like
an organism, call the study a morphology. If the object is *becoming* one,
the honest word is the committed one. This article sets out to earn it.

## The word that will not hedge

Begin with the sentence *The Shape of Humanity* set aside. In the second
message, on a base «relatively near the earth», Yahweh lays out the
cosmology the project calls
[*The Infinite in Both Directions*](/articles/the-infinite-in-both-directions/)
— a universe {% wiki(slug="infinity") %}infinite{% end %} up and down the
scales, in which the stars are the atoms of a gigantic being and the atoms
of your arm hold inhabited worlds. In the middle of it he states an axiom
that the fractal article read as physics and that this one reads as
biology:

{% library(book="extraterrestrials-took-me-to-their-planet", chapter=2, verse=32) %}
But all in the universe is living and is in harmony with the infinitely
large and the infinitely small. The earth is living, like all the planets,
and, for the little mould that is humanity, it is difficult for you to
realize it because of the time lag due to the enormous difference of mass
which prevents you from capturing its palpitations.
{% end %}

*All in the universe is living.* Not *is like something living*, not *can
usefully be modeled as living* — is living. The {% wiki(slug="mass-effect") %}Mass
Effect{% end %} explains why we cannot feel it: the Earth's clock runs too
slow for ours to register its palpitations, exactly as an elephant's runs
too slow for a fly. But the ontology underneath the physics is a straight
biological assertion, and it does not stop at the planet. Humanity itself,
in the canon, is «the little mould» on the living Earth — a film of small
living things on the surface of a larger one — and, one rung further in, a
single organism in its own right, «a great being in gestation» whose cells
are persons.

This is why the name matters. A *morphology* would be an honest
under-description of a canon that says its object is alive. To call the
discipline a morphology, having read that «the earth is living, like all
the planets», is to flinch at the exact moment the source does not. The
canon is not shy about the strong claim. It is the source of the strong
claim. The only question is whether the strong claim can be defended in the
open, next to the biology — and that is a question about biology's own
account of how a crowd of living things turns into one living thing.

## How one becomes many-into-one

Here is the thing the mainstream is supposed to make impossible, and
does not. Biology has a rigorous, non-mystical, entirely orthodox account
of a colony of individuals fusing into a single higher individual. It has
watched it happen. It happened to make *us*.

The idea is old enough to have a founding paper. In 1911 the Harvard
entomologist William Morton Wheeler published «The Ant-Colony as an
Organism» and argued that an ant colony is not a metaphor for a body but a
body in the relevant sense — it feeds, grows, defends itself, reproduces as
a unit, and dies as a unit, while its constituent ants are as functionally
committed to it, and as unable to persist outside it, as the cells of a
tissue. A queen is a germ line; the workers are a soma that will never
reproduce; the colony is the individual that natural selection actually
sees. Nearly a century later Bert Hölldobler and Edward O. Wilson — Wilson
the founder of sociobiology, both of them among the most decorated
students of social insects who ever lived — gave the case its mature form
in a book they simply titled *The Superorganism*, and made the deflationary
point that no analogy is being stretched: the ant colony passes the same
tests of individuality that a wolf or an oak passes, so a science that
called the wolf an organism and the colony a metaphor would be applying two
standards to one fact.

Wheeler's colony is a special case of a far larger pattern, and the
pattern is the hinge of this article. In *The Major Transitions in
Evolution* (1995), the evolutionary biologists John Maynard Smith and Eörs
Szathmáry proposed that the whole history of life is punctuated by events
of one specific kind: entities that once reproduced independently become
parts of a larger entity that reproduces as a whole, and in doing so
surrender the ability to go it alone.{{ footnote(id="4") }} Free-living
bacteria were engulfed and became the mitochondria inside every cell of
your body — a fusion the biologist Lynn Margulis spent her career proving,
against long resistance, so that the eukaryotic cell is now known to be
itself a permanent community of once-separate organisms. Single cells that
could have lived alone bound their fates together into multicellular
bodies, most of their number giving up reproduction forever to become
liver and bone and skin so that a sequestered few could carry the line
onward. Solitary wasps became the eusocial colony. At every step the same
signature: independence traded for membership, the many becoming a one that
selection can act on directly, a new *individual* appearing one level up
the ladder — the very ladder the fractal cosmology climbs without end.

So the deep objection — *surely a crowd of separate organisms cannot
really be a single organism* — is not an objection biology recognizes. Of
course it can. It is the standard way big individuals get built, and you
are made of two such fusions stacked on top of each other: a cell that is a
fused colony of microbes, inside a body that is a fused colony of cells.
The Raëlian claim that humanity is a third such fusion — a fused colony of
persons — is not a category error. It is the next term in a sequence
biology already accepts. What it needs is not permission but a *test*: has
the fusion actually happened, or is it a poetic anticipation of something
still to come?

Biology supplies the test. David Queller and Joan Strassmann, working on
the evolution of social behavior, proposed that «organism» is the wrong
yes-or-no question and «organismality» the right graded one: an assemblage
is organismal to the degree that its parts *cooperate* highly and
*conflict* little.{{ footnote(id="5") }} A human body scores near the
ceiling — its trillion cells almost never defect, and cancer, the defection
that does occur, is precisely what the body polices hardest. An ant colony
scores high. A slime mould that aggregates only under stress scores lower;
a herd, lower still. The philosopher of biology Peter Godfrey-Smith
describes the same passage from the other side, as a *de-Darwinizing* of
the parts: as a whole tightens into an individual, its components stop
being separate players in the evolutionary game and become organs of a
single player. Both frameworks say the same useful thing. Individuality is
not a light switch. It is a dimmer, and a transition in progress is exactly
what you see when the dial is turning but has not yet reached the top.

That is the frame this Explainer will apply to the present. But first it
has to deal with the one place biology has tried this move at the planetary
scale before, and failed — because the failure is instructive, and because
the canon happens to hold the missing piece.

## The Gaia problem

There is already a famous hypothesis that the Earth is an organism, and it
is famous partly for having been shot down. In the 1970s the independent
British scientist James Lovelock, working with Lynn Margulis, proposed
**Gaia**: that the biosphere does not merely sit on the planet's surface
but actively *regulates* it, holding the temperature, the oxygen, the ocean
chemistry within the narrow bands life requires and far from where dead
chemistry would settle — behaving, in short, like the homeostasis of a
single vast body.{{ footnote(id="6") }} It was a serious scientific claim
with serious evidence behind it: the atmosphere really is a wild chemical
disequilibrium that something keeps propped up, the way a living body keeps
its temperature propped up against the cold.

Then the evolutionists came for it, and their objection was fatal in its
simplicity. Adaptations — the appearance of design *for* something — are
built by natural selection, and natural selection needs a population:
many entities of the same kind, reproducing with variation, the
better-adapted out-reproducing the worse until the trait spreads. The molecular
evolutionary biologist W. Ford Doolittle put it plainly in 1981, and
Richard Dawkins sharpened it in *The Extended Phenotype* a year later:
there is only one Earth. It has no parents, no offspring, and no rivals. No
population of planets ever competed, the well-regulated ones leaving more
daughter-planets than the badly-regulated ones. So whatever holds the
Earth's chemistry in its narrow band, it cannot be an *adaptation* built by
selection for the planet's own good, because the machinery that builds such
things was never running at the planetary scale. Gaia's regulation, on this
verdict, has to be an accident or a byproduct — real, perhaps, but not the
homeostasis of a genuine organism, because a genuine organism is a thing
selection made, and selection cannot have made this.

This is the same wall *The Shape of Humanity* ran into from the direction
of history — the wall of the single specimen, the sample of one, the object
of which there is only ever one to examine — now built out of evolutionary
theory instead of methodology. And it is a real wall. An honest
macrobiology cannot climb over it by waving at atmospheric chemistry and
calling the planet alive. If the Earth is an organism in any sense stronger
than «a system with feedback loops», there has to be an answer to Doolittle
and Dawkins: there has to be a population, and a selective history, and a
breeder.

The canon's contribution is to supply all three.

## The breeder the biology lacked

Everything the Gaia hypothesis could not have — a population of planets, a
reproduction with variation, a selection operating on whole living worlds —
is stated outright in the second message, not as poetry but as the Elohim's
own working method. The comparison across worlds is the premise the Wheel of
Heaven project calls {% wiki(slug="cosmic-competition") %}Cosmic
Competition{% end %}, and it rests on two sentences. The first names the
population and the breeder. The Elohim are a
{% wiki(slug="life-engineering") %}life-engineering{% end %} civilization
that has done this many times:

{% library(book="lets-welcome-the-extraterrestrials", chapter=2, verse=105) %}
In the same way, we who are used to creating life on an infinity of
planets, know what happens to a Humanity which has reached your level of
technology, without having reached an equivalent level of wisdom.
{% end %}

There is the population Gaia never had. Not one Earth but a great many,
seeded deliberately across «an infinity of planets» by an agency that keeps
doing it — reproduction with variation, in the only form a planet-organism
could ever have it, since planets do not bud daughters but *can* be
copied, one civilization creating the conditions of life on the next, each
run differing in its starting parameters. And the second sentence names the
selection, in language a Darwinist would recognize on sight:

{% library(book="extraterrestrials-took-me-to-their-planet", chapter=2, verse=25) %}
This is, in a way, a natural selection, on the cosmic scale, of the species
capable of escaping their planet. Only those who perfectly master their
aggressiveness can attain this stage. The others self-destruct as soon as
their scientific and technological level permits them to invent weapons
powerful enough for that.
{% end %}

*A natural selection, on the cosmic scale.* This is precisely the missing
machinery. Doolittle and Dawkins said the Earth cannot be an organism
because no population of planets undergoes differential reproduction. The
canon answers that there *is* such a population — the humanities seeded
across the cosmos — and that it *does* undergo differential reproduction,
filtered by a selection pressure stated with total specificity: the
capacity to master aggression before acquiring the power to self-destruct.
The worlds that pass propagate onward into the
{% wiki(slug="cosmic-chain") %}cosmic chain{% end %}, inheriting the science
and, in time, seeding worlds of their own. The worlds that fail are removed
from the population by their own hand. That is not a metaphor for selection.
It is a description of selection, with a substrate (planetary humanities), a
variation (their differing wisdom-to-power ratios), and a filter (the
maturity test). The canon rescues the planet-organism from the exact
objection that killed Gaia, and it does so with the one resource biology
could not go out and find for itself: a breeder, standing outside the
specimen, with a population of specimens and a record of how they run.

This is worth stating precisely, because it is the strongest thing the
canon contributes to the discipline. Gaia was the right hypothesis crippled
by a missing premise. The premise it was missing is the central factual
claim of the Raëlian cosmology. Whether that claim is *true* is exactly the
speculative wager this whole project is built to examine in the open — but
its logical role is not vague. It is the specific piece the science of
planetary organisms has always needed and never had. Macrobiology, on the
canon's terms, is possible precisely where Gaia was impossible, and for
precisely the reason Gaia failed.

## Reading the threshold

Grant the frame, and the present acquires a strange, clarifying pressure.
Apply Queller and Strassmann's dimmer-switch — high cooperation, low
conflict — to humanity now, and what shows up is not a finished organism
but a transition visibly in progress, the dial turning in real time.

A great deal has switched on in a single human lifetime. The planet grew
a **nervous system**: a communication net that now carries the signals of
eight billion cells across the whole body at the speed of light, where
eighty years ago there was nothing of the kind. It runs a single
planet-spanning **metabolism** — supply chains that move matter and energy
around the globe as a circulatory system moves them around a body, so
tightly coupled that a blockage in one strait or one factory town is felt
in every organ within days. The economic geographer's version of this,
Geoffrey West's finding that cities scale *superlinearly* and race toward a
crisis that forces either transformation or collapse, is the growth curve
of a body approaching a threshold. And the archaeologist Joseph Tainter's
cold observation, quoted at length in the companion Explainer — that
«collapse, if and when it comes again, will this time be global», that «no
longer can any individual nation collapse» — is the clinical sign that the
parts have fused past the point of independent survival. A body that can no
longer lose a limb and live is the definition of an individual. By that
test the fusion is already far along.

But the dial has not reached the top, and the canon names the exact
variable still holding it back. Cooperation is high and climbing; *conflict*
is not yet low. The one thing a completed transition in individuality
requires — the suppression of defection among the parts, the reason your
cells almost never turn cancer and your colony's workers almost never lay
eggs — is in humanity still unfinished, and its unfinished name in the
canon is aggression. The selection filter of the second message is a
statement about organismality in Queller and Strassmann's own terms:
the worlds that complete the transition are the ones whose parts stop
fighting each other in time; the ones that do not, self-destruct at the
moment their power outruns their cohesion
({% libref(book="extraterrestrials-took-me-to-their-planet", chapter=2, verse=25) %}ETTMTTP 2:25{% end %}).
The canon's «demons» — «aggressiveness against one's fellows or against the
nature in which one lives» — are cancer read at the planetary scale: the
defection of the part against the whole, arriving just as the whole acquires
the power to make that defection fatal to everyone.

Which means the failure mode has a biological name too. When the transition
does not complete — when conflict is not mastered before the singularity —
the organism does not mature. It miscarries. The canon calls this
{% wiki(slug="samsara") %}saṃsāra{% end %}, the wheel: self-destruction
followed by «a new slow progression from the state of primitive towards
that of an evolved people», the gestation begun again on this world or
another. *The Shape of Humanity* follows that dark edge to its end and this
Explainer will not repeat the journey; the point to carry here is only that
the two vocabularies have converged. A completed major transition in
individuality, or the {% wiki(slug="golden-age") %}Golden Age{% end %}. A
transition that fails to suppress internal conflict before it matters, or
saṃsāra. Biology and the canon are describing one process, and both say the
same unsettling thing about where we stand in it: near the hinge, and not
yet safe.

## The cross-examination

A thesis that ends by calling human aggression «cancer at the planetary
scale» has earned some hostile questions, and there are two witnesses who
must be called. The first attacks the biology. The second remembers the
history.

The first witness is the evolutionist who killed Gaia, and he has not
finished. *You have not earned the word*, he says. *You have done the very
thing your own companion article warned against — you have put the
aliveness into the name and called that an argument. Superorganism is a
lens, not a fact; a beehive is not literally an animal; and your*
«natural selection on the cosmic scale» *rests entirely on taking the
messages' word for a population of planets no telescope will ever show me.
Strip that unverifiable premise and you are back to a single Earth, a
single humanity, an analogy — a morphology after all, wearing a biologist's
coat it did not earn.*

The charge lands, and the honest reply is a concession that splits the
discipline in two. There is a **this-world macrobiology** that needs no
Elohim at all: the study of the transition in individuality we can actually
observe on Earth, using Wheeler and Maynard Smith and Szathmáry and Queller
and Strassmann, measuring the rising cooperation and the stubborn conflict,
reading the nervous system and the metabolism and the fused-fate of the
parts. That science is fully mainstream-compatible, and it does not require
believing a word of the canon; it requires only taking seriously that
humanity may be undergoing the same kind of event that made the eukaryotic
cell. And there is a **canonical macrobiology** that adds the breeder, the
population of worlds, and the pass-mark — and *that* science is speculative
in the project's strict sense, because its load-bearing premise is exactly
the claim about the Elohim that cannot be checked from inside the specimen.
This Explainer carries the **speculative** tag for that reason, and means
it. The claim that humanity is *already* an organism is not established.
The defensible claim is narrower and still large: that humanity is
*becoming* one, by a process biology recognizes and has a test for; that
the test currently returns «transition in progress, not complete»; and that
the canon supplies, if its cosmology is granted, the precise missing premise
that would turn the planetary version of the claim from an accident into a
science. Where the confirmed biology ends and the granted premise begins is
a seam this article has tried to keep visible, not to paper over. Name the
seam honestly and the word *macrobiology* is earned for the near side of it
outright, and offered as a wager on the far side — which is all the canon
ever asks of anyone.

The second witness is graver, and this project has faced him before. The
idea that a society is a body and a person is a cell is not a harmless
figure; it is one of the most dangerous ideas human beings have ever
had, and it has a body count. Thomas Hobbes drew the commonwealth as «an
Artificial Man» in 1651 and made the sovereign its soul. Herbert Spencer
built a whole sociology on the organism analogy in the nineteenth century.
And in the twentieth, the metaphor was weaponized — the individual reduced
to tissue, the «health of the body» made a warrant for spending persons
like cells — until Karl Popper spent *The Open Society and Its Enemies*
dismantling the organic theory of the state root and branch. A macrobiology
that forgets this has forgotten the most important thing about its own
central image.{{ footnote(id="7") }}

It is worth noting that even Spencer, the analogy's great popularizer, drew
the line the canon draws. Unlike a real body, he insisted, the social
organism has no single sentience over and above its members, and it exists
*for* them, not they for it — «society exists for the benefit of its
members, not its members for the benefit of society». That is the whole
guardrail in one sentence, and the canon states it in its own idiom. The
Infinite is «infinitely indifferent» to us; no cosmic body is owed our
sacrifice; the individual's life «has only the importance that we give
it», a value assigned from within and not extracted from above. The
organism this discipline describes is one that matures *by* the awakening
and flourishing of its cells, one at a time, freely — the exact inverse of
the totalitarian body that matures by consuming them. *To describe a whole
is not to license the dissolution of its parts.* A macrobiology that keeps
that distinction bright is doing something biology already does without
scandal: it describes the wolf without concluding that the wolf may eat its
own cells for sport. The danger is real, and the answer to it is not to
refuse the science but to build it under the discipline that the danger
demands.

## What has never happened before

Return to the man o' war. Its zooids will never know they became an animal.
The float has no way to learn that it is a float; the stinger cannot step
back far enough to see the whole drifting colony it serves. Every major
transition in the history of life — the bacteria that became mitochondria,
the cells that became bodies, the wasps that became the hive — was made in
exactly this blindness, by parts that could not perceive the individual
they were fusing into, because to perceive it they would have had to stand
outside a thing they were made of and smaller than.

There is one difference in the case this Explainer is about, and it is the
whole of the case. This transition is the first in the history of life that
its own parts can see happening. A cell in the forming body of humanity can
do what no mitochondrion and no worker ant has ever done: it can notice the
body, name the process, run the test, and — this is the part with no
precedent anywhere in biology — decide whether to complete the transition
or abort it. The canon's «one chance in a hundred» is not a fact about a
blind mechanism. It is a fact about a mechanism that has, for the first
time, become aware of itself through the very nervous system it grew, and
can therefore push on its own odds. Every previous individual in the tree of
life was assembled without consent. This one is being asked for it.

That is why the name should commit. To call the study of a blind fusion a
*morphology* — a reading of shape — would be adequate, because a blind
fusion has only a shape to read. But this fusion has more than a shape. It
has, uniquely, the capacity to know what it is and to choose whether to
become it, which is to say it has the one property we have never been able
to withhold from the word *life*: it acts, at its own scale, on its own
behalf. A science of that is not a morphology. It is a biology of the
largest living thing we are part of, studied from the inside by cells that
have just learned they are cells — and the right name for it is the one
that refuses to look away from what its object is doing. Its object is
coming alive at a new scale. Call the study by the fact. Call it
macrobiology.
