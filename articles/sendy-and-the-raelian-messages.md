In Jean Sendy's account of {% wiki(slug="eden") %}Eden{% end %}, forbidden knowledge is kept in a laboratory. Adam and Eve enter it, consult books and illustrations, and discover that their celestial teachers have withheld something essential about their own possibilities. The {% wiki(slug="serpent") %}serpent{% end %} who encourages them loses his prospect of joining the beings from the sky and must remain on Earth. Readers of {% libref(book="the-book-which-tells-the-truth") %}the first Raëlian Message{% end %} will recognize this combination: scientific books, forbidden instruction, and a punishment understood as terrestrial exile. The resemblance extends well beyond a general interest in visitors from other worlds.{{ cite(id="1", title="Ces dieux, chapters 28–29") }}{{ cite(id="2", title="The Book Which Tells the Truth, 2:28–39") }}

Read a little further, however, and the participants change. Sendy's serpent in this account is an indigenous human undergoing experimental transformation. The Message's serpent is a group of creators who sympathize with their human creations. Sendy's visitors intervene in a world whose living beings have evolved naturally, whereas the Message makes the succession of terrestrial life the work of designers.

Some skeptics, including former Raëlians, have drawn attention to these parallels and encouraged readers to compare the books. Their accounts are useful starting points because they point to passages that can be checked. A list of coincidences, however, cannot show how each passage works. A shared image may conceal different mechanisms, and a philosophical difference may sit beside a remarkably close reading of the same biblical sentence.{{ cite(id="7", title="Former Raëlian testimony: comparison list") }}{{ cite(id="8", title="Additional former Raëlian testimony: reading Sendy and listed parallels") }}

{% wiki(slug="jean-sendy") %}Jean Sendy{% end %} and {% wiki(slug="rael") %}Raël{% end %} share several particular ways of turning biblical narrative into technological history, enough to make selective adaptation a serious possibility. Their books also differ in biological assumptions, historical imagination, and literary purpose, enough to repay reading as distinct projects. Both observations hold at once. A work can transform borrowed material extensively, and a resemblance can also arise through an intermediary or a common source.

Wheel of Heaven reads the comparative material through the {% wiki(slug="reference-corpus-of-the-raelian-canon") %}Raëlian canon{% end %}. That commitment supplies no historical evidence about how either book was composed. The questions here concern what the texts say, how their explanations work, and what their agreements can reasonably establish. The truth of the reported encounters is a separate matter.

## The texts being compared

The central comparison is between Sendy's *Ces dieux qui firent le ciel et la terre*, first published in 1969, and Raël's *Le Livre qui dit la vérité*, published in 1974 and presenting an encounter dated December 1973. Sendy's *Nous autres, gens du Moyen Âge* appeared in 1969; *L'ère du Verseau* followed in 1970. Together, these books provide more of his intellectual framework than any one passage about the Elohim can convey. *Les temps messianiques*, published in 1975, belongs to the subsequent discussion and includes his apparent response to Raël.{{ cite(id="1", title="Ces dieux: publication and inspected edition") }}{{ cite(id="3", title="L’ère du Verseau: publication and inspected edition") }}{{ cite(id="4", title="Nous autres: publication and inspected edition") }}{{ cite(id="5", title="Les temps messianiques, chapter 17") }} Sendy's life, his earlier *Cahiers de cours de Moïse*, and his lunar wager are treated in the companion article [*The Man Who Bet the Bible on the Moon*](/articles/the-man-who-bet-the-bible-on-the-moon/). The present article concentrates on passage-level comparison.

The comparison uses French source texts. Sendy's supplied digital editions include later reissues; the *Ces dieux* EPUB identifies a 1979 J'ai Lu edition. The French Message used here represents the 2005 collected text in the project's digitization. None of these witnesses has been collated against a first printing. Their wording shows what the inspected texts contain, but an original publication date does not automatically date every sentence in a later edition. Attributing a given detail to a pre-1974 printing would require that collation, which matters most for any argument about borrowing.

**Quotations from the first Message use the Library’s existing English translation**, with links to the corresponding paragraphs. Sendy’s French excerpts are followed by English translations made for this article. The comparison was checked against the French texts. Because the two sets of English renderings come from different translators, they cannot show identical wording between the authors, and they may make the authors sound closer or further apart than they are. Chapter numbers locate Sendy’s passages; references such as *2:30* identify paragraphs in the project’s digitization of the first Message, rather than biblical verses. Biblical references are named separately.

The first Message receives most of the attention because it is the earliest Raëlian book and contains the principal Genesis comparisons. Where the article speaks of the Message's narrative, it means this first book. Its findings do not extend to the presence or absence of a theme elsewhere in the Raëlian canon, and later teachings are not read back into the first book's composition.

The comparison works at three levels. The first is the shared premise: biblical beings understood as technologically advanced visitors. The second is the particular interpretation, such as {% wiki(slug="tree-of-the-knowledge-of-good-and-evil") %}forbidden fruit{% end %} as books or a tower as a spaceflight project. The third is the role that interpretation plays in the surrounding story. Agreement at the second level can be striking even when the third differs greatly, and most of what follows concerns the relation between those two levels.

## Elohim and the human scale of the divine

Both authors place the beings called {% wiki(slug="elohim") %}Elohim{% end %} within a world of bodies, technical capacities, and practical decisions. These beings can travel, conduct experiments, preserve knowledge, make mistakes, and face consequences. Their superiority to terrestrial humans depends on an unequal development of knowledge. Humanity can therefore hope to acquire abilities that once appeared divine.

The first Message establishes the relationship in its encounter dialogue:{{ cite(id="2", title="The Book Which Tells the Truth, 1:53") }}

{% library(book="the-book-which-tells-the-truth", chapter=1, verse=53) %}
We are men like you, and we live on a planet quite similar to Earth.
{% end %}

The speaker proceeds to describe life on another planet resembling Earth. His physical presence is the narrative's first assurance that the beings of the subsequent biblical exposition belong to an intelligible material world. Before Genesis is interpreted, the reader has already met someone presented as a member of the civilization that will explain it.

Sendy arrives at comparable beings through reconstruction. In *L'ère du Verseau*, he asks readers to adopt a galactic perspective. Earth is one inhabited world among possible others, and terrestrial humans belong within that larger category. The label *Galaxiens*, a form of {% wiki(slug="cosmic-pluralism") %}cosmic pluralism{% end %}, helps him resist the assumption that humanity occupies one fundamental category and every other intelligent civilization another. In his argument, similarities of form can reflect the operation of broadly shared natural laws.{{ cite(id="3", title="L’ère du Verseau, chapter 1") }}

The resemblance in physical form therefore rests on different explanations. Sendy's naturally evolved inhabitants can resemble other inhabitants of the galaxy, with later biological intervention sharpening the relationship. The Message explains humanity's likeness to its creators through deliberate manufacture, so that the biblical image of being made in their likeness becomes an account of design. Comparable bodies come with different genealogies.

There is a parallel in how both works approach biblical translation. Sendy repeatedly restores a plurality of celestial agents where conventional French wording gives readers a singular *Dieu*. The Message likewise makes the plurality of Elohim central to its reinterpretation and assigns the term a celestial meaning. In each work, a change in translation opens the way to a change in historical explanation. A divine command becomes an instruction; creation becomes technical work; apparent supernatural intervention becomes the action of a civilization.{{ cite(id="1", title="Ces dieux, chapters 1 and 9") }}{{ cite(id="2", title="The Book Which Tells the Truth, 2:2–5") }}

This is an agreement between two readings. It does not verify their proposed Hebrew meanings, whose grammar, usage, and contexts require separate linguistic examination.

Sendy also anticipates a detail of the later Raëlian account of the Elohim's leadership. In *La lune, clé de la Bible* (1968), he treats {% wiki(slug="yahweh") %}Iahvé{% end %} mainly as an immaterial Principle, comparable to Carnot's principle in thermodynamics, but notes that the biblical Iahvé constantly acts as « le patron des Elohim ». He offers, for what it is worth, a further possibility: the Elohim might call their hereditary king or elected president « Iahvé ». He then calls Iahvé the weak point of his thesis, because several explanations seem equally convincing. The first Message introduces the president of the {% wiki(slug="council-of-the-eternals") %}Council of the Eternals{% end %}, who says he directed the creation of life on Earth, without naming him. The {% libref(book="extraterrestrials-took-me-to-their-planet") %}second Message{% end %} (1975) is the one that calls this figure Iahvé.{{ cite(id="10", title="La lune, clé de la Bible, chapter 9") }}{{ cite(id="2", title="The Book Which Tells the Truth, 7:56") }}

Sendy's treatment also has an older intellectual ancestry within his own presentation. *Ces dieux* invokes Voltaire's discussion of the plural at the beginning of Genesis. *L'ère du Verseau* draws on the {% wiki(slug="kabbalah") %}Zohar{% end %}, Philo, and medieval interpreters when discussing divine likeness and the relation between celestial beings and an immaterial principle. These references are part of the structure of his argument, even where his use of them would need independent examination.{{ cite(id="1", title="Ces dieux, opening dedication and chapter 9") }}{{ cite(id="3", title="L’ère du Verseau, chapter 16") }}

The first Message generally concentrates this interpretive authority in its reported speaker. Readers are told which biblical passages matter and how to understand them. Sendy's references invite a reader to follow a chain of interpretation; the Message's dialogue presents an explanation from a participant in the history. From the first pages, the two books ask for trust on different grounds.

## Eden: a laboratory, with different starting material

The laboratory is one of the clearest shared settings. Both works connect Eden with organized biological activity and explain the knowledge gap between humans and their celestial counterparts through access to science. The differences begin with what the researchers find on Earth and what they are trying to do there.

In *Ces dieux*, the Earth already has a long biological history. A catastrophe has disrupted its conditions. The celestial researchers work with indigenous material to restore species, re-establish ecological relationships, and conduct experiments. Sendy describes their activity as:

> *« le travail de recherche, en laboratoire, sur le matériel génétique indigène »*
>
> Laboratory research on indigenous genetic material.
>
> — Sendy, *Ces dieux*, chapter 18{{ cite(id="1", title="Ces dieux, chapter 18") }}

The phrase *indigenous genetic material* determines the whole setting. There is something terrestrial to recover and modify. The researchers' exceptional ability consists in reading, reconstructing, and directing a history that began before their arrival. Sendy can accordingly imagine frozen remains, surviving populations, restored organisms, and experimental changes within a larger ecological recovery.

The Message describes the initial biological work differently:{{ cite(id="2", title="The Book Which Tells the Truth, 2:16") }}

{% library(book="the-book-which-tells-the-truth", chapter=2, verse=16) %}
Then, in this magnificent and gigantic laboratory, they created plant cells from nothing other than chemical products.
{% end %}

Here the terrestrial laboratory is the setting for making living forms. The creators have already conducted experiments on their own planet, where public alarm and government prohibition give them a reason to continue elsewhere. Earth offers a suitable site. Their work develops through increasingly elaborate forms, eventually reaching humans made in their likeness.{{ cite(id="2", title="The Book Which Tells the Truth, 2:2 and 2:16–28") }}

Both passages use the language of the laboratory, but they give it different work. Sendy's reconstruction depends on a damaged biosphere and prior terrestrial evolution. The Message depends on a project of biological creation transferred from another world. Earth's earlier inhabitants matter to an account of recovery in a way they cannot matter to an account of manufacture.

The distinction reaches even a small scene from Genesis, the naming of animals. In chapter 18, Sendy treats the celestial researchers' dealings with the human as resembling visitors learning indigenous vocabulary. The animals already belong to a terrestrial world whose language the newcomers need to acquire. In the Message, the creators teach humans the names and uses of the surrounding plants and animals. The direction of instruction reverses.{{ cite(id="1", title="Ces dieux, chapter 18: the naming of animals") }}{{ cite(id="2", title="The Book Which Tells the Truth, 2:31–32") }}

A list of parallels would record only that both authors interpret the naming scene scientifically. In one account, visitors learn from the people already there; in the other, creators instruct their own creations.

The order of the work is similar. In chapter 9 of *Ces dieux*, the gods first restore light, then order, then restart « le cycle de vie des plantes, puis celui des herbivores, ne redonnant vie aux carnivores qu'une fois leur nourriture assurée » (the life cycle of plants, then of herbivores, bringing back carnivores only once their food was secured). The Message's creators likewise move from plants to aquatic life and then to land animals. They create algae for small fish and larger fish to eat smaller ones, so that "a natural balance" is established, which the speaker calls ecology. Genesis already supplies the sequence of days. Both authors add the same ecological reason for it.{{ cite(id="1", title="Ces dieux, chapter 9") }}{{ cite(id="2", title="The Book Which Tells the Truth, 2:16–24") }}

Chapter 24 of *Ces dieux* goes further, in the voice of the celestial scientists themselves. They speak of making the females of the indigenous biped mutate so that they can be fertilized « par la semence des dieux ». They describe the resulting « demi-dieux » as more quickly intelligent than humans. And they say that a humorous god nicknamed the experimental population of Eden « le peuple élu ». The Message's exiled creators take the most beautiful daughters of men as wives and have exceptional children with them, reading {% libref(book="genesis", chapter=6, verse=4) %}Genesis 6:4{% end %} as proof. It also traces the people of Israel to the most brilliant team of creators and calls them the most intelligent people. Genesis 6 supplies the bare plot of divine beings and human women for both authors. The joining of hybrid offspring, intelligence, and an "elect" population produced by experiment is a further shared element.{{ cite(id="1", title="Ces dieux, chapter 24: demi-dieux and le peuple élu") }}{{ cite(id="2", title="The Book Which Tells the Truth, 2:28, 2:51–55 and 2:72") }}

Their experiments also take place under different social conditions. Sendy's researchers enjoy unusual freedom from the financial and administrative constraints he associates with terrestrial science. He imagines a small scientific community with extensive control over its environment and subjects. The Message begins with researchers constrained by public concern on their home planet, and later makes disagreement among creators and decisions by their government central to the plot.{{ cite(id="1", title="Ces dieux, chapters 19 and 24") }}{{ cite(id="2", title="The Book Which Tells the Truth, 2:2,28–39") }}

Both accounts permit a troubling distance between experimenter and subject. Humans can be treated as material whose intelligence must be measured and whose knowledge must be limited. The texts give that inequality different histories, but in both the laboratory ties the question of education to the question of control. The tie is tested when the forbidden knowledge is disclosed.

## Evolution: an open question and an answer

Sendy's position on evolution is easily misrepresented by selective quotation. He criticizes explanations of how particular organs developed, quotes objections to the explanatory power of mutation, and makes provocative statements in a polemical voice. Taken out of context, these can suggest a wholesale rejection of evolution. His explicit statements about life's history say otherwise.

Chapter 2 of *Ces dieux* says:

> *« Il est probable que l’espèce humaine est le produit d’une lente évolution, à partir des formes de Vie élémentaires. »*
>
> It is probable that the human species is the product of a slow evolution from elementary forms of life.
>
> — Sendy, *Ces dieux*, chapter 2{{ cite(id="1", title="Ces dieux, chapter 2") }}

Later in the same chapter he treats the principle of evolution from elementary life towards more complex and differentiated forms as no longer seriously contested, and then asks how that development occurred. Here he offers the image that comes closest to the Message's whole account of biology:

> *« la Vie apparaît avoir évolué exactement comme elle évoluerait dans un laboratoire où, à partir de l’« étincelle initiale » expérimentalement obtenue, des biologistes chercheraient à créer, par tâtonnements successifs, les formes vivantes les plus complexes […] sans craindre les audaces débouchant sur des formes monstrueuses »*
>
> Life appears to have evolved exactly as it would evolve in a laboratory where, starting from an experimentally obtained ‘initial spark’, biologists sought to create, by successive trial and error, the most complex living forms […] without fearing audacities that led to monstrous forms.
>
> — Sendy, *Ces dieux*, chapter 2{{ cite(id="1", title="Ces dieux, chapter 2: the laboratory analogy") }}

Sendy does not draw the conclusion this image invites. He quotes Émile Guyénot's argument that no known mutation has produced a new organ and asks where new organs come from. He then considers a Creator who experiments, produces monsters, and tries again, and sets it aside as anthropomorphism. He rejects with equal force the "natural cybernetics" proposed by Albert Ducrocq, calling both options articles of faith. His own answer is a deliberate suspension of judgment: he has nothing better to propose, and the scientifically defensible position is to say that he does not know. The chapter ends by asking whether humanity received the "helping hand" that the first civilizations claimed.{{ cite(id="1", title="Ces dieux, chapter 2: Guyénot, Ducrocq, and suspended judgment") }}

Sendy's scientific authorities belong to the biology of his own time, and his objections are no guide to present-day evolutionary science. The literary question is what his visitors do with life's history. In *Ces dieux*, naturally evolved organisms precede their arrival and become the objects of their intervention. The analogy of a trial-and-error laboratory describes how the record *looks*; Sendy leaves open who or what, if anyone, did the experimenting.

The first Message presents another account under the heading *L'évolution : un mythe*. It preserves a succession from simpler to more complex forms but assigns the progression to the creators' technical development. A short formulation makes the relationship clear:{{ cite(id="2", title="The Book Which Tells the Truth, 5:61–65") }}

{% library(book="the-book-which-tells-the-truth", chapter=5, verse=64) %}
The evolution of the forms of life on Earth is the evolution of the techniques of creation and the sophistication of the works realized by the creators in order finally to arrive at the creation of a being similar to them.
{% end %}

The surrounding explanation grants that scientists have recognized aspects of the succession. Its objection concerns the cause. Improved methods of design account for increasingly elaborate organisms, while earlier human forms become prototypes superseded by later designs. Biological resemblance records the history of technical work.

Set beside Sendy's chapter, the Message reads as an answer to the question he declined to settle. Both describe the record of life as a sequence of trials, including failed and monstrous forms (the Message's creators also mention earlier monsters on their own planet, 2:2). Sendy offers the laboratory as a comparison for an evolution whose cause he leaves open; the Message makes the laboratory literal and names the experimenters. The analogy is close enough to count among the significant resemblances between the books. The conclusions drawn from it differ: in Sendy, an independently developing living world later receives a civilization's intervention, while in the Message the succession itself is the creators' work.

A sentence in *L'ère du Verseau* complicates the picture. Sendy declares that only a medieval obscurantist could accept that humans descend from a fish. Taken alone, it sounds like an anti-evolutionary slogan. But the immediate discussion has already described intelligent technological beings as the outcome of evolution, and Sendy repeatedly places himself among the supposedly medieval thinkers he is defending. His reversal of conventional praise and insult makes the sentence's irony relevant to its meaning.{{ cite(id="3", title="L’ère du Verseau, chapter 18") }}

The provocation has to be weighed against the explicit evolutionary passages. *Nous autres* supplies further evidence: its opening describes a materialist understanding of the celestial beings as inhabitants of a world where life arose earlier and reached spaceflight through natural evolution. That explanation belongs to Sendy's account of his own intellectual camp.{{ cite(id="4", title="Nous autres, opening: materialist and spiritualist medievalists") }}

The authors' criticisms of scientific certainty resemble each other as well. Both resist an account of life they find insufficient and use contemporary science to reopen an older text. A borrowing hypothesis would not need to explain away the difference on evolution: the step from Sendy's analogy to the Message's literal laboratory is short. It would need to explain why the Message drops the long natural history that Sendy keeps.

## Forbidden knowledge: books behind a prohibition

The interpretation of the forbidden fruit is among the strongest specific parallels. Both authors convert eating into acquiring knowledge and place written scientific information at the centre of the event. The prohibition protects an unequal relationship between those who possess knowledge and those expected to remain dependent on them.

Sendy's reconstruction in chapter 29 makes the setting unusually concrete:

> *« s’ils entraient dans ce laboratoire, s’ils « mangeaient » les livres et gravures qui s’y trouvent »*
>
> If they entered this laboratory, if they ‘ate’ the books and illustrations found there.
>
> — Sendy, *Ces dieux*, chapter 29{{ cite(id="1", title="Ces dieux, chapter 29") }}

The warning that they will die prevents access to information. When they enter and survive, they discover both the knowledge and the falsity of the warning. Their relationship with their teachers changes because the authority that sustained their obedience has been damaged. Sendy gives the episode the structure of an experiment disrupted by information reaching its control group.

The Message's explanation is comparably direct:{{ cite(id="2", title="The Book Which Tells the Truth, 2:29–30") }}

{% library(book="the-book-which-tells-the-truth", chapter=2, verse=30) %}
Which means: you may learn everything you want, read all the books we have here at your disposal, but do not touch the scientific books or you will die.
{% end %}

The young humans are allowed a broad education while being denied the knowledge that would make them potential rivals. The surrounding paragraphs distinguish useful instruction about plants and animals from dangerous scientific instruction. Once the prohibition is broken, humans understand that they too can become creators and resent being treated as laboratory animals.{{ cite(id="2", title="The Book Which Tells the Truth, 2:31–37") }}

The common interpretation contains several connected choices. Eating becomes learning. A tree becomes a source or location of knowledge. A death warning becomes a means of restricting access. The knowledge acquired reveals that the celestial teachers possess attainable capacities. These choices form a more particular resemblance than the idea of advanced visitors alone.

The common biblical text accounts for part of this. Genesis already links eating with knowledge and with becoming like the beings who prohibit it. The metaphor of consuming instruction appears elsewhere in biblical literature, including the {% libref(book="ezekiel", chapter=3, verse=1) %}eaten scroll in Ezekiel{% end %}. Neither author had to invent the association between food and knowledge. What is distinctive is the placing of scientific books inside a laboratory, combined with the other interpretations nearby.

Sendy's version gives considerable attention to the experimental consequences. His human subjects have been divided and prepared for different purposes. Once previously uninformed subjects acquire the forbidden understanding, the experiment can no longer proceed under its earlier conditions. The loss is a failure of managed development: the researchers have lost the ignorance on which their procedure depended.

The Message develops the emotional and political consequences. Some scientists want the humans to receive fuller instruction because they love them. Other authorities fear what an educated creation could become. The educational dispute becomes a disagreement among the creators, and the authorities on their planet impose punishment. Knowledge passes through an act of sympathy whose consequences extend beyond the laboratory.

In both versions, the biblical account becomes a story about the management of technical inequality. A child or subject can develop into a peer, and the superior group must decide whether to encourage that development. In Sendy the story turns on an experiment disrupted; in the Message it turns on a conflict over the education of beloved creations.

## The serpent: a similar sentence, a changing identity

The serpent's role has several components: who it is, what it knows, why it shares the knowledge, who punishes it, and what the punishment means. The two works agree closely about the last component and differ about the others.

In chapter 29 of *Ces dieux*, Sendy proposes an indigenous human being undergoing transformation. The next chapter names this figure {% wiki(slug="lucifer") %}Lucifer{% end %}, « le plus intelligent des créés » (the most intelligent of the created), to whom the Celestials had meant to entrust the planet. The figure knows more than the humans who serve as the experimental control group and cannot resist showing that knowledge. The designation *serpent* becomes an insult directed at this more advanced subject. The penalty makes sense because the figure had the prospect of becoming celestial and loses it.

Sendy describes the resulting condition with the phrase:

> *« condamné à rester cloué au sol »*
>
> Condemned to remain pinned to the ground.
>
> — Sendy, *Ces dieux*, chapter 29{{ cite(id="1", title="Ces dieux, chapter 29") }}

His argument depends on the inadequacy of punishing an ordinary snake by making it crawl. A creature already moving along the ground would acquire little new disability from that sentence. A human expected to join the celestial community, however, loses a future by being restricted to terrestrial existence.

The Message identifies the serpent explicitly:{{ cite(id="2", title="The Book Which Tells the Truth, 2:35–39") }}

{% library(book="the-book-which-tells-the-truth", chapter=2, verse=39) %}
The “serpent”—that small group of creators who had wanted to teach Adam and Eve the truth—was condemned by the government of the original planet to live on Earth in exile, while the other creators had to stop their experiments and leave the Earth.
{% end %}

The group's members already belong to the advanced civilization, and their punishment is imposed by the government of their original planet. They remain on Earth in exile while the other creators must leave. The penalty is again loss of access to the celestial home, but the relation between punishment and status has changed. Sendy's experimental subject loses an ascent that has not yet happened. The Message's dissenting scientists lose the right to return to a home they already have. The difference alters who is responsible for what.

The motives differ in these passages as well. The figure in *Ces dieux* displays knowledge to impress less advanced companions. The Message's group acts out of attachment to its creations and a wish to educate them fully. Both break a restriction for different reasons, and the Message's serpent reads as a sympathetic faction within a divided scientific community.

Sendy himself complicates the contrast. In chapter 17 of *L'ère du Verseau*, the celestial group degenerates through too frequent mixing with the "mortal" element, until one of its own, Lucifer-{% wiki(slug="satan") %}Satan{% end %}, leads humanity's ancestor into "sin". The remaining members need centuries to reconstitute a "divine race" able to depart, « amputés de Lucifer-Satan laissé sur Terre » (without Lucifer-Satan, who is left on Earth). This is close to the Message's exile of the serpent group on Earth. That formulation comes closer to a dissident celestial than the experimental human of *Ces dieux*.{{ cite(id="3", title="L’ère du Verseau, chapter 17: Lucifer-Satan and departure") }}

The following chapter makes the educational parallel closer still:

> *« Lucifer veut porter la lumière aux hommes tout de suite. Satan veut que les hommes sachent tout, tout de suite. »*
>
> Lucifer wants to bring light to humans immediately. Satan wants humans to know everything, immediately.
>
> — Sendy, *L’ère du Verseau*, chapter 18{{ cite(id="3", title="L’ère du Verseau, chapter 18: premature disclosure") }}

Sendy identifies this figure as a Théosite, a member of the visiting civilization. The disagreement concerns whether humanity is ready for full disclosure. His reconstructed argument defends gradual preparation and treats the disclosure as premature; the Message foregrounds the scientists' love for their creations. The shared educational purpose is substantial here, while the judgment of timing and responsibility differs. A comparison restricted to the experimental human of *Ces dieux* would miss this closer precedent.

Sendy's own accounts of the serpent therefore vary from book to book. A critic cannot say that his serpent is always an indigenous mutant, and a defender of the Message cannot treat that identity as a decisive separation from Raël. Each comparison has to name the book it draws on, since the closest parallel may lie in a different volume from the most convenient counterexample.

What persists across these variations is the reading of crawling as restriction to Earth. The agents change while the device remains. Because it adapts so easily, it is a plausible element for transmission between works, and for the same reason the identity of the serpent cannot settle the question of dependence.

## Noah: preserving life in a transportable form

The {% wiki(slug="great-flood") %}Flood{% end %} comparison combines a shared problem with a shared technological direction. Preserving living diversity inside a vessel becomes easier to imagine when the cargo is reduced to reproductive or biological material. Both authors reject a straightforward picture of every animal boarding intact. Their explanations connect survival with the capacity to regenerate life.

Sendy discusses the Hebrew term *tebah*, its use for {% wiki(slug="noahs-ark") %}Noah's vessel{% end %} and the container associated with the infant Moses, and the possibility of applying it to a space capsule. He then writes:

> *« ce que l’Épopée de Gilgamesh appelle « les semences de vie » »*
>
> What the Epic of Gilgamesh calls ‘the seeds of life.’
>
> — Sendy, *Ces dieux*, chapter 31{{ cite(id="1", title="Ces dieux, chapter 31") }}

The appeal to Gilgamesh is Sendy's, and his wording has not been checked here against an edition of the epic. What matters for the comparison is that his Noah preserves a reduced form of living inheritance, together with knowledge and equipment.

The Message specifies a vehicle and a journey:{{ cite(id="2", title="The Book Which Tells the Truth, 2:58") }}

{% library(book="the-book-which-tells-the-truth", chapter=2, verse=58) %}
But the exiles, forewarned of the matter, had asked Noah to build a rocket that was to orbit the Earth during the cataclysm, containing a couple of each species to be safeguarded. This is an image. In reality—and your scientific knowledge will soon allow you to understand it—it is enough to have a living cell of each species, male and female, to then reconstitute the whole being.
{% end %}

The same paragraph explains the biological preservation through living cells, and {% libref(book="the-book-which-tells-the-truth", chapter=2, verse=68) %}paragraph 2:68{% end %} describes the recreation of animals from the preserved material. This is an explicit orbital evacuation followed by biological reconstruction.

The decision that makes the rescue necessary is shared more closely than the vehicle. In *Ces dieux*, the Lord of the Celestials decides to suppress every form of life on Earth ({% libref(book="genesis", chapter=6, verse=7) %}Genesis 6:7{% end %}) before Noah finds favour. Sendy later summarizes the biblical chronology: « les Célestes décident de tout détruire sur Terre », then help Noah build his *tebah*, and leave, setting off the Flood "by some delayed-action device". He compares the destruction of Eden to « un super-Hiroshima ». The Message's distant planet decides to destroy its creation with nuclear missiles, and exiles who have been warned help Noah. In both books, then, the Flood is a deliberate technological destruction ordered by the celestial authority. The Message adds a factional structure, with the home government on one side and the exiled scientists on the other. Sendy's Celestials act as one body.{{ cite(id="1", title="Ces dieux, chapters 30–31 and 34: the decision to destroy and the delayed Flood") }}{{ cite(id="2", title="The Book Which Tells the Truth, 2:55–58") }}

Sendy's capsule analogy stops short of that itinerary. In the cited chapter, Noah prepares the vessel, takes shelter, and emerges when conditions have returned to normal. The comparison with Apollo makes technological enclosure part of the interpretation, but the inspected passage does not have Noah orbit the Earth.

The cargo differs in precision as well. *Seeds of life* is a suggestive and fairly broad expression, whereas cells from each species to be restored give the Message a specific mechanism.

Noah's function extends beyond species survival in Sendy. He preserves the teachings that will enable later generations to interpret their past and pursue the knowledge of the departed visitors. The vessel carries the means of cultural continuity as well as biological continuity. Sendy calls Noah the point of contact across the Flood between humanity and its celestial teachers.

That role becomes necessary because the visitors leave. After their departure, humanity's access to them must depend on what survived. The equipment, texts, instructions, and descendants form a continuous inheritance. Sendy's subsequent account of {% wiki(slug="prophet") %}prophets{% end %} and ancient civilizations grows out of this preservation scene.

The Message has less need to make Noah the sole connection to an inaccessible past because its creators remain capable of intervening. They observe Earth's recovery, respond to later human projects, and continue communicating through selected representatives. Noah belongs within an ongoing relationship whose participants can act again.{{ cite(id="1", title="Ces dieux, chapters 31 and 34") }}{{ cite(id="2", title="The Book Which Tells the Truth, 2:58,68,72–78") }}

The biological cargo is a substantial point of similarity, and the scene serves each history differently. Sendy needs an inheritance capable of surviving the teachers' absence. The Message needs a rescue that explains how a threatened creation survives within a continuing conflict.

## Babel: a failed ascent and an interrupted ascent

Both authors place the {% wiki(slug="tower-of-babel") %}tower{% end %} within the history of attempts to reach the sky. Both connect its failure with the dispersal of knowledge and the loss of coordinated human activity. Their explanations of the failure are markedly different.

In chapter 35 of *Ces dieux*, Sendy asks why builders seeking literal height would cross mountainous country and choose a plain. He takes the choice of site as a clue that the structure was intended to support a launch mechanism. The proposed machine is primitive, an enlarged application of a familiar mechanical principle:

> *« une sorte de socle, d’ancêtre dinosaurien de l’arbalète »*
>
> A kind of base, a dinosaur-sized ancestor of the crossbow.
>
> — Sendy, *Ces dieux*, chapter 35{{ cite(id="1", title="Ces dieux, chapter 35") }}

The aim is to reach the Moon, where Sendy's wider argument places a surviving celestial inheritance. The builders have enough tradition to conceive the objective but lack the means to accomplish it. Their confidence exceeds their technical capacity. Failure then generates blame, disputes about the inherited texts, and an inability to cooperate.

For Sendy, this supplies a natural explanation of the confusion of languages. No returning visitor is required to sabotage the project. The undertaking defeats itself through its prematurity, and the participants disperse after they cease to understand one another. The biblical attribution to a celestial speaker is interpreted through the continued operation of laws and inherited instructions.

The Message gives Babel another form and destination:{{ cite(id="2", title="The Book Which Tells the Truth, 2:72–76") }}

{% library(book="the-book-which-tells-the-truth", chapter=2, verse=72) %}
The latter wanted men to go to the creators’ planet in order to obtain their forgiveness by showing that men were intelligent and scientific but grateful and peaceful. So they built an immense rocket: the Tower of Babel.
{% end %}

Its builders are assisted by the exiled creators. They hope to reach the creators' planet and obtain pardon by demonstrating that humanity has become scientifically capable, peaceful, and grateful. The project expresses the possibility of reconciliation between an advanced creation and its fearful creators.

Its failure follows a deliberate response. Observers on the original planet discover the project, intervene, disperse the most knowledgeable people among populations with different languages, and destroy the equipment. The confusion follows the distribution of experts across communities where they cannot communicate effectively. The tower is halted because the other civilization fears what the achievement could mean.

Several differences can now be stated precisely. Sendy's destination is the Moon; the Message's is the creators' planet. Sendy's mechanism is an inadequate primitive launcher; the Message's is a major rocket built with knowledgeable assistance. Sendy's failure follows technical limits and social disintegration; the Message's follows external action. Sendy's absent teachers remain present through inheritance; the Message's creators remain present through surveillance and force.

Babel thus gathers several disagreements that appear separately elsewhere: about spaceflight, the availability of celestial intervention, the reliability of preserved knowledge, and the relation between technical progress and political fear. The summary *Babel was a rocket* captures the shared reading of an attempt on the sky and drops the destination, the builders' motive, and the cause of failure. A possible source can supply an idea without supplying any of these.

## Departure, death, and the possibility of renewed contact

Sendy's original visitors have a much more definite absence than the phrase *ancient astronauts* might suggest. In *Ces dieux*, they depart after leaving Noah an inheritance. The later chapter on Babel makes their eventual fate explicit:

> *« Les Célestes qui ont laissé leur enseignement à Noé sont défunts depuis quelques millénaires. »*
>
> The Celestials who left their teaching to Noah have been dead for a few millennia.
>
> — Sendy, *Ces dieux*, chapter 35{{ cite(id="1", title="Ces dieux, chapter 35") }}

He immediately proposes that their descendants live in some neighbouring planetary system. In this reconstruction the original personnel are dead, while the wider lineage need not be extinct. To say that Sendy's Elohim no longer exist would suppress the descendants he expressly allows; to say only that they left would understate what he says about the individuals who taught Noah.

The practical consequence is that later prophets cannot depend on conversations with the original visitors. In chapter 34, Sendy makes renewed terrestrial visits extremely improbable. He reasons that an intervention by beings with such a technical advantage would have produced effects too large to leave their presence doubtful. He similarly rejects the explanation that later prophets received regular advice by radio. An adviser with that level of knowledge could have supplied conspicuous practical advantages.{{ cite(id="1", title="Ces dieux, chapter 34") }}

His alternative is the interpretation of documents. A prophet can attribute something to the Elohim because it comes from their inherited teaching. Sendy compares this with an author saying that a text tells him something. In the Babel chapter, he extends the analogy to the way a long-deceased writer can still speak through a book or a scientific principle can still govern events. The language of communication survives without a new conversation.

The first Message explains the decreasing visibility of the creators through a continuing choice to intervene discreetly. {% libref(book="the-book-which-tells-the-truth", chapter=3, verse=113) %}Paragraph 3:113{% end %} describes their reduced appearances and increased use of unobtrusive communication. Its prophets remain representatives of beings who can still act, observe, and confer authority, even when they are absent from ordinary view.{{ cite(id="2", title="The Book Which Tells the Truth, 3:113") }}

That relationship is already present in the opening encounter. The visitor claims prior observation of Claude Vorilhon and presents the meeting as an intended contact. The later commission then turns it towards the future: humanity must demonstrate that a public arrival can occur safely. An {% wiki(slug="embassy") %}embassy{% end %} becomes the material expression of readiness for that arrival.{{ cite(id="2", title="The Book Which Tells the Truth, 1:15–24; 5:56,66; 6:21–22") }}

The disagreement reaches into the authors' imaginative treatment of travel. Sendy devotes extensive attention to voyages lasting generations, closed ecological systems, and messages that take centuries to reach another world. In chapter 24, even communication with the original planetary civilization becomes a sequence of reports rather than a practical conversation. Distance imposes a form on social life.{{ cite(id="1", title="Ces dieux, chapter 24") }}

In the Message's opening dialogue, the answer to the travel-time question is radically compressed:{{ cite(id="2", title="The Book Which Tells the Truth, 1:54–55") }}

{% library(book="the-book-which-tells-the-truth", chapter=1, verse=55) %}
As long as it takes to think of it.
{% end %}

The exchange leaves the mechanism unexplained. Its literary effect is to make interplanetary presence available on a scale wholly different from Sendy's generational journeys. For Sendy, the demands of travel help account for inherited duties, biological continuity, and the rarity of return. In the Message, the visitor's arrival makes the continuing relationship immediate.

These assumptions cannot be exchanged without changing the stories. Remove continuing observation and intervention from the Message, and the present commission loses its speakers. Give Sendy's prophets routine access to their teachers, and his long history of preserved and misunderstood documents no longer has a reason to exist. Even where the authors agree about an advanced civilization's ancient activity, they place humanity in different relationships with it now.

## What survives when the beings are called eternal

Both works discuss eternity in a vocabulary that includes biology. The agreement is partly verbal, because *eternal* may refer to life, matter, a lineage, an individual, or the preservation of a particular personality. Only the mechanism shows which is meant.

Chapter 24 of *Ces dieux* imagines the celestial scientists speaking about themselves. The chapter is particularly useful because it allows Sendy to explore their outlook from inside the reconstruction. Their time horizon extends over many generations, and their sense of identity is bound to the continuity of their community and genetic inheritance.

The closed environment of an interstellar vessel intensifies this continuity. Matter circulates through food, bodies, waste, and renewed life. Parents die; their descendants continue the undertaking; the physical materials remain within the system. Sendy gives this a deliberately unsettling psychological meaning. Knowing oneself as part of an enduring biological and material process requires a different understanding of ambition, mortality, and purpose.

The imagined speaker eventually says:

> *« Comme le temps passe… Je vais bientôt mourir. »*
>
> How time passes… I shall soon die.
>
> And, at the chapter’s close:
>
> *« Bah ! Mes cellules verront cela. »*
>
> Well! My cells will see it.
>
> — Sendy, *Ces dieux*, chapter 24{{ cite(id="1", title="Ces dieux, chapter 24") }}

The speaker expects his own death and takes consolation in continuity beyond it. Earlier passages describe successive generations and the maintenance of an unusually stable biological line. His use of *I* stretches across a collective history, and he remarks on the difficulty ordinary humans have in understanding that claim.

Sendy's imagined biology includes highly controlled reproduction and assumptions about hereditary stability, which raise scientific and ethical questions of their own. For the comparison they clarify what he means here: the persistence of a lineage and its material constituents, together with a trained psychological acceptance of that persistence. The chapter does not describe the preservation of one individual's accumulated memories.

The first Message's explanation of recreation specifically includes:{{ cite(id="2", title="The Book Which Tells the Truth, 7:29–31") }}

{% library(book="the-book-which-tells-the-truth", chapter=7, verse=30) %}
When we truly die, from a cell taken from the little piece of our body that was removed earlier, we completely recreate the body such as it was at that moment. I do say such as it was at that moment, that is, with all its scientific knowledge and its personality of then.
{% end %}

Its speaker describes a body reconstructed from preserved material taken during an earlier period of life. The resulting person retains the knowledge and personality of that period. The continuity being asserted is personal and biographical: a particular individual receives another embodied existence with the attributes that identify that individual.

The following paragraph adds selection. A council judges who deserves another life, and repeated lives can lead to permanent membership. Population limits and restrictions on reproduction belong to the account. Biological capacity thus becomes part of a moral and institutional order. The possibility of survival is connected with what an individual does and how that life is evaluated.

The emotional implications differ accordingly. Sendy's speaker must reconcile himself to individual death within a process that continues. The Message offers selected individuals renewed personal existence. A reader hoping to continue as the same person would receive different answers from the two passages, though both speak of cells and of eternity.

There is a shared concern beneath that difference: how beings with a very long horizon find reasons to act. Sendy's scientific community turns towards undertakings whose completion lies beyond a single life. The Message associates advanced knowledge with creation, happiness, and the continuation of humanity across worlds. Both imagine that technical achievement raises questions about purpose once ordinary material needs have ceased to dominate.{{ cite(id="1", title="Ces dieux, chapter 24") }}{{ cite(id="2", title="The Book Which Tells the Truth, 5:57–60 and 7:30–31") }}

An author could take up the prospect of biological continuity without accepting Sendy's psychological account of it. The presence of a cell in two explanations does not mean that both describe the same survival.

## Sendy's medievalism and the inheritance of knowledge

Sendy's medievalism is central to both the value and the difficulty of his work. His books repeatedly argue that a tradition preserved possibilities that later humanism dismissed, and that modern technical achievements reopen access to them. Within his argument, spaceflight vindicates an older conception of humanity's place in the universe.

The title *Nous autres, gens du Moyen Âge* establishes the position from which he speaks. He adopts the identity commonly used as a term of intellectual disparagement and turns it into a claim to foresight. In its opening, medieval thinkers are imagined as preserving the expectation that humans could fly, communicate at a distance, transform matter, and eventually equal the celestial teachers. The subsequent humanist triumph becomes a period in which that inheritance is obscured.{{ cite(id="4", title="Nous autres, opening De profundis") }}

His characterization is strongly polemical. He treats humanism as a conviction that humanity owns the planet and occupies the supreme position, and links it with commercial acquisition and misplaced certainty. His praise of the medieval world accompanies sweeping judgments about religions and civilizations. These judgments are Sendy's construction of intellectual history, and they are reported here as his, not as an account of what medieval people or humanists actually believed.

For this comparison, the question is what that construction allows his books to do. It gives Sendy a historical community, a language of inheritance, and a reason to search traditional texts for knowledge that their later readers have forgotten how to interpret. His reading of Genesis becomes one part of a much larger argument about the survival and recovery of understanding.

The opening of *Nous autres* describes the visitors as those who:

> *« en quittant la Terre, ont laissé la Tradition »*
>
> On leaving Earth, left the Tradition.
>
> — Sendy, *Nous autres*, opening discussion of Tradition{{ cite(id="4", title="Nous autres, opening discussion of Tradition") }}

*Tradition* names a body of transmitted teaching whose origin he attributes to the departing visitors. It must survive their absence through texts, practices, interpreters, and institutions. The difficulties of its transmission become part of the explanation for the mixture of insight and obscurity in inherited culture.

Sendy makes room within this medievalist position for both spiritualists and materialists. Spiritualists can understand the celestial beings as emissaries of God. Materialists can understand them as spacefarers from a world whose natural development began earlier. Their agreement concerns the reality of the teachers and the inheritance they left. The cosmological interpretation of those teachers can remain disputed.

The position is more flexible than the familiar picture of Sendy as a writer who replaced God with astronauts. In *L'ère du Verseau*, an immaterial principle still belongs to the discussion, alongside concrete celestial beings. The question of those beings' existence does not exhaust the question of ultimate order. His references to the Zohar and medieval thinkers help articulate that distinction.{{ cite(id="3", title="L’ère du Verseau, chapter 16") }}

The Message organizes its explanation differently. Its reported encounter supplies a present authority who identifies the relevant past and gives instructions for the future. Readers can recognize the Bible's concealed technological meaning because the creators explain it to a messenger. The opening gives that messenger's lack of scientific and literary specialization a positive role: he should convey the explanation simply.{{ cite(id="2", title="The Book Which Tells the Truth, 1:44–46") }}

Sendy's reader, by comparison, moves through arguments about texts, archaeology, science, intellectual history, and the anticipated discovery of material remains. His prose continually introduces interlocutors: the conventional believer, the confident skeptic, the specialist, the physicist, the initiated reader, the humanist. The author tries to reposition them within the history he is reconstructing.

The two works therefore read very differently. Sendy often circles around a question, stages objections, and uses irony to reverse the reader's expectations. His medievalist self-description can make an apparently dismissive adjective function as praise. The Message's dialogue usually moves towards a determinate explanation and a practical consequence. Its speaker supplies the answer that will authorize the next stage of the mission.

The affinity is genuine. Both works suggest that the past contains information modern assumptions have obscured, both expect scientific achievement to make older narratives newly intelligible, and both refuse to place humanity at the summit of intelligence. Sendy's account of the medieval inheritance nonetheless has its own commitments and argumentative machinery, which disappear if he is read as an earlier version of Raël.

His medievalism is also a reason to read the books for their own sake. They are sustained experiments in relating science to tradition, with provocative assumptions and identifiable weaknesses, and their interest does not depend on whether a later writer used them.

## Humanity's tenure of Earth

The overlap does not stop at Genesis. Sendy's opening attack on ownership has a recognizable counterpart in the first Message's social programme. Both deny that humanity possesses an unrestricted title to the world and use a vocabulary of conditional occupancy.

In *Nous autres*, Sendy writes:

> *« NOUS AUTRES, GENS DU MOYEN AGE, PENSONS N’ÊTRE QUE DES CONCESSIONNAIRES. »*
>
> We people of the Middle Ages consider ourselves merely holders of a concession.
>
> — Sendy, *Nous autres*, opening{{ cite(id="4", title="Nous autres, opening: concessionnaires") }}

A concession-holder has a right of use within a prior order. Sendy's formulation places humans within an inheritance whose terms they did not create. It supports his wider rejection of humanity as the absolute measure and proprietor of everything around it. His medievalism supplies the intellectual home for that relationship.

The Message uses a closely related image:{{ cite(id="2", title="The Book Which Tells the Truth, 6:7") }}

{% library(book="the-book-which-tells-the-truth", chapter=6, verse=7) %}
This world is not yours; that too is written in the Bible. You are only its tenants. Thus, all goods must be leased for forty-nine years. That abolishes the injustice of inheritances.
{% end %}

The paragraph moves from unequal inheritance and wasted talent to a proposal that goods be leased for forty-nine years. Its concern is the organization of human society: how to reduce inherited inequality while preserving incentives for effort and discovery. The image of tenancy becomes a specific institutional proposal.

The overlap is philosophical as well as broadly verbal. Both make absolute ownership difficult to justify, and both connect the use of Earth with obligations larger than individual possession. Their reasons and applications differ. Sendy's concession belongs to the relation between humanity and an inherited cosmic order; the Message's tenancy becomes an instrument for changing the distribution of opportunity.

The Message itself invokes biblical authority for the proposition, so the comparison also points towards a shared scriptural background. An argument that this political idea came specifically from Sendy would need to examine that background and other possible intermediaries, since a biblical principle cannot simply be credited to the most recent author who used it.

The example still matters. It rules out an easy division of the books into borrowed origins and wholly independent ethics. Some philosophical concerns overlap, while others, especially Sendy's medievalist account of intellectual history, remain his own.

## Aquarius and the scale of history

Both authors locate their readers at a threshold where humanity's technical capacities make older promises intelligible. The [Age of Aquarius](/timeline/age-of-aquarius/) supplies a vocabulary for that threshold, though a shared age is easily mistaken for agreement about every date attached to it.

In *L'ère du Verseau*, Sendy connects the beginning of Aquarius with the period around 1950 and the developing ability to repeat actions attributed to the celestial beings. He distinguishes a zodiacal sign from a constellation in explaining his choice. Elsewhere in the same discussion, the days of Genesis become units of 2,160 years of the {% wiki(slug="precession") %}precession of the equinoxes{% end %} within a larger chronological scheme. These periods allow him to align stages of the biblical narrative with his reconstruction of earthly history.{{ cite(id="3", title="L’ère du Verseau, chapter 18: days of 2,160 years and Aquarius") }}

The first Message also identifies a contemporary Aquarian transition. {% libref(book="the-book-which-tells-the-truth", chapter=5, verse=4) %}Paragraph 5:4{% end %} gives 1946 as the beginning and associates the present with the disclosure of truth. It also speaks of twenty-two thousand years since the creators decided to undertake their work on Earth. A later passage describes the knowledge they could share as giving humanity an advantage of twenty-five thousand years.{{ cite(id="2", title="The Book Which Tells the Truth, 5:4 and 5:56") }}

One of these figures matches Sendy exactly. In *L'ère du Verseau*, and already in *La lune, clé de la Bible*, Sendy places the arrival of the celestial visitors around 22,000 years ago, at the Würm III glaciation that he identifies with the *tohu-bohu* of Genesis 1:2: « le texte biblique affirme que des bipèdes mammifères ainsi faits sont arrivés sur Terre, il y a 22 000 ans ». The Message's twenty-two thousand years count from the creators' decision to begin their work on Earth, not from an arrival after a glaciation, but the number is the same. It belongs among the specific agreements between the books. The twenty-five thousand years of 5:56 measure something else, a technical lead, and should not be read as a second date. Sendy also begins Aquarius around 1950, where the Message gives 1946.{{ cite(id="3", title="L’ère du Verseau, chapter 17: arrival around 22,000 years ago") }}{{ cite(id="10", title="La lune, clé de la Bible: Würm III and the arrival") }}

The common historical pattern is clearer than any numerical alignment. Both works imagine modern technical achievement as a recovery of capacities once associated with the sky beings. Spaceflight, communication at a distance, and the practical transformation of matter change what readers consider possible. Ancient descriptions become newly legible when the present begins to resemble them.

Sendy's expected confirmation is tied strongly to the discovery of a surviving inheritance, especially on the Moon. His contemporary threshold promises access to evidence and knowledge left behind. The Message's threshold opens a relationship with living speakers who judge whether humanity is ready to receive assistance. One expects to recover what was left; the other expects to be told more. (Sendy's anticipated lunar discoveries are reported here as part of his argument; quoting the expectation does not establish that it was fulfilled.)

Both books nonetheless use time in the same way. They make contemporary humanity responsible for what comes next and place the reader between an obscure inheritance and a possible expansion of knowledge. That shared placement helps explain why the books can feel familiar to one another even where their cosmologies diverge.

## Reconstruction, testimony, and the reader's task

Sendy announces his literary method in the subtitle of *Ces dieux*: *le roman de la Bible*. He explains the choice by comparing reconstruction from fragmentary evidence with the work of a naturalist restoring an extinct animal from its surviving bones. Imagination supplies connections between fragments, and later discoveries could vindicate or undo those connections. He explicitly acknowledges that his own bridges may be weaker than those of the scientific exemplar he invokes.{{ cite(id="1", title="Ces dieux, chapter 1") }}

The term *roman* marks the imaginative work needed to produce a coherent account from incomplete evidence; Sendy still treats the historical question as real. His confidence frequently grows as the reconstruction unfolds, and the prose can become forceful and dismissive of opponents. Yet the initial distinction between evidence and reconstructed scene remains part of the declared method.

A concise formulation in *Nous autres* gives the reader a test:

> *« je propose une hypothèse dont la vérification expérimentale est imminente »*
>
> I propose a hypothesis whose experimental verification is imminent.
>
> — Sendy, *Nous autres*, opening statement of method{{ cite(id="4", title="Nous autres, opening statement of method") }}

The expectation is ambitious, and it exposes the argument to a test beyond internal coherence: what material discovery would actually support the reconstruction? The hypothetical framing does not excuse every unsupported historical assertion in the books, but it names the kind of warrant Sendy wanted his argument eventually to have.

The first Message presents another route to authority:{{ cite(id="2", title="The Book Which Tells the Truth, 1:44") }}

{% library(book="the-book-which-tells-the-truth", chapter=1, verse=44) %}
You will write down everything I tell you and have the book containing these writings published.
{% end %}

The speaker commands a written transmission. The author becomes recipient and messenger, and the explanations draw their authority from the speaker's participation in the history they describe. The first-person encounter is the reason readers are asked to take the exposition as more than an interpretive proposal.

The book's later instructions give that transmission a practical destination. They specify a residence, rooms for visitors, a conference space, a landing terrace, privacy, and recognition of the site as an embassy. The explanation of humanity's past becomes preparation for a future meeting, and the reader is invited to support an organized undertaking.{{ cite(id="2", title="The Book Which Tells the Truth, 6:21–22") }}

Sendy's books ask readers to reconsider an inheritance and to follow an argument whose confirmation lies in future discovery. The Message asks readers to accept a reported communication and to take part in its consequences. One develops a speculative historical and philosophical tradition; the other delivers an encounter account that founds a mission. A later author could move an explanation from an essayist's argument into the mouth of a celestial speaker without changing much of its content, so this difference in authority bears on the books' purposes more than on their relationship.

## A comparison of the principal mechanisms

The main results can be set alongside one another without collapsing their differences:

| Question | Sendy's inspected accounts | The first Message |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Where does terrestrial life come from? | Natural evolution precedes celestial restoration and intervention. | The sequence of life reflects successive biological designs. |
| What is Eden? | A scientific settlement working on indigenous life and selected human subjects. | A centre of creation and instruction, with dangerous knowledge withheld. |
| What is forbidden? | Entry into a laboratory and acquisition of knowledge from books and illustrations. | Access to scientific books that would enable humans to become creators. |
| Who is the serpent? | An experimental human in *Ces dieux*; a celestial dissident appears in *L'ère du Verseau*. | A sympathetic group of creators that educates its creations. |
| What does its punishment mean? | Loss of celestial possibilities and confinement to Earth. | Terrestrial exile imposed on dissenting creators. |
| What does Noah preserve? | Biological seeds, equipment, and the inherited teaching. | Cells for recreation, preserved aboard an orbiting rocket. |
| Why does Babel fail? | A premature launch project exceeds human capacity and ends in disputes. | The creators intervene, disperse experts, and destroy equipment. |
| How do later prophets receive instruction? | They interpret the documents inherited from departed teachers. | The creators continue communicating discreetly. |
| Who remains available for contact? | *Ces dieux* says the original visitors died; their descendants may live elsewhere, while humanity inherits the teaching. | Living creators observe humanity and commission a present messenger; this comparison does not establish their identity with the original personnel. |
| What is eternal? | In the examined passage, a lineage and the continuity of life and material. | Selected individuals can be recreated with knowledge and personality. |
| What place does medievalism have? | It organizes a history of preserved knowledge and humanist forgetting. | The encounter and its commission organize the explanation. |
| What does humanity possess? | A conditional concession within an inherited order. | A tenancy translated into a programme of forty-nine-year leases. |
| How far does the biblical reading extend? | Concentrated on Genesis 1–11, with brief passes over Abraham, Moses, and precessional symbolism; Ezekiel's vision is denied as a sighting of cosmonauts in *L'ère*. | Genesis followed by a continuous reading through Exodus, Judges, Samuel, Kings, the prophets, Job, the Psalms, and the Gospels. |
| What comes next? | Expected recovery of a celestial inheritance and confirmation of the hypothesis. | Preparation for a public meeting and an embassy. |

Some rows describe close correspondences; others concern incompatible mechanisms. Scoring each row as a match or a difference would hide their unequal weight. A laboratory is a broad motif. Forbidden scientific books followed by celestial exclusion form a more particular combination. A different explanation of Babel's failure changes the plot more deeply than a change of vehicle terminology.

## Chronology: eleven years of books and a young journalist

Any theory of how the two bodies of work are related has to fit their dates. The dates also put the comparison in proportion: one author worked on his thesis across a decade of books, and the other produced his book within a year of the encounter it reports.

Sendy, born in 1910, published *Les cahiers de cours de Moïse* in 1963, in his early fifties. *Les dieux nous sont nés* followed in 1966, *La lune, clé de la Bible* in 1968, then *Nous autres, gens du Moyen Âge* and *Ces dieux qui firent le ciel et la terre* in 1969, and *L'ère du Verseau* in 1970. English translations of three of these appeared between 1972 and 1975. By 1973 he had spent more than ten years developing a single reading of Genesis across six books, alongside lectures and broadcasts. He died on 24 April 1978.{{ cite(id="6", title="Bigliardi: Sendy's bibliography and biography") }}

{% wiki(slug="rael") %}Claude Vorilhon{% end %} was born on 30 September 1946. His own account of his life before 1973, published with the second Message, describes Catholic boarding schools, little interest in study, and leaving school at fifteen to hitchhike to Paris with a guitar. He then spent several years as a cabaret singer and recording artist. After a serious road accident and a failed attempt to join a magazine venture in Dijon, he settled in Clermont-Ferrand and founded a motor-sport magazine. He spent the next three years testing cars and racing. The account says nothing about reading in ancient-astronaut literature, esotericism, or biblical criticism. It is the account of the Message's own author, and it can neither confirm nor exclude such reading.{{ cite(id="11", title="Extra-Terrestrials Took Me to Their Planet, 1:7–61") }}

The encounter reported in the first Message took place on 13 December 1973, when Vorilhon was twenty-seven. The second Message describes what followed. In the first days he put his notes in order, taken "far too quickly" while the speaker talked. He sent the manuscript to a Paris publisher, appeared on Jacques Chancel's television programme *Le Grand Échiquier*, and recovered the manuscript only with difficulty. He then gave up his motor-sport magazine, and the book came off the press "à l'automne 1974". Vorilhon turned twenty-eight on 30 September of that year, so he was twenty-seven when he wrote the book and twenty-eight, or very nearly, when it was printed. The date of the television broadcast has not been verified for this article.{{ cite(id="11", title="Extra-Terrestrials Took Me to Their Planet, 1:63–70") }}

Between the encounter and the printing, Sendy spoke in Clermont-Ferrand itself. Bigliardi cites a report in *La Montagne* dated 5 March 1974 of Sendy's appearance at the first national science-fiction congress in the city. There Sendy dismissed recent reports of extraterrestrial encounters, arguing that anyone who had really met extraterrestrials would have gained "an awesome power". The report has not been inspected here, and nothing shows that Vorilhon attended. Whether Sendy's remark was aimed at Vorilhon's recent television appearance is an open question. In any case the lecture comes after the encounter date the Message reports.{{ cite(id="6", title="Bigliardi, section 13") }}

Sendy's sentence about a young lecturer who had read his books appeared in *Les temps messianiques* in 1975. The second Message, with Vorilhon's autobiography, followed in 1975–76.

The chronology permits several explanations. For direct borrowing, Vorilhon would have had to read Sendy before or during the writing of a manuscript completed within months of December 1973. Sendy's books were widely available, so that is possible. The chronology alone can neither establish nor exclude it. It does show the difference in scale between the two authors' preparation, at least as each presents it. Sendy came to 1973 with a decade of published argument. Vorilhon's account of himself mentions no prior study of the subject.

## Beyond Genesis: what the first Message adds

Most published comparisons, including the lists circulated by skeptics, concentrate on Genesis. That is where the parallels are, and it is also where the comparison has to begin. But Genesis occupies one chapter of the first Message. Its longest chapter, on the "surveillance of the elect", reads the history of Israel from {% wiki(slug="moses") %}Moses{% end %} to the prophets, and the following chapters turn to {% wiki(slug="jesus") %}Jesus{% end %}, the parables, and the prophecies of the end. A blueprint hypothesis has to account for this material too.

For this article, every passage that the first Message quotes, paraphrases, or reinterprets was inventoried from the French text: 226 items, 209 of them biblical. Each item was then searched for in the five Sendy books published before 1974 (*Les cahiers de cours de Moïse*, *La lune, clé de la Bible*, *Ces dieux*, *Nous autres*, and *L'ère du Verseau*). Each was classified by the closest treatment in any of them: the same specific reading, a related reading with a different mechanism or purpose, a mention without the Message's kind of reading, or no treatment at all.

The results divide sharply by chapter. In the Message's Genesis chapter, every one of 44 items has some counterpart in Sendy: 13 with the same reading and 27 with a related one. In the two chapters that follow, on Israel and the prophets and on Jesus, 105 of 147 items have no counterpart in these books at all. Twenty-nine more are mentioned by Sendy without the Message's kind of reading, and only 13 receive a similar or identical one. Isaiah is quoted in eighteen items in the Message and receives a comparable reading in Sendy only once. Judges, Kings, Daniel, Jonah, and almost all of the Gospel material are absent.

A few examples show what this material looks like. The {% wiki(slug="ark-of-the-covenant") %}ark of the covenant{% end %} becomes a transmitter-receiver with its own atomic power source, used to communicate when the creators were too far away for telepathy. The idol Dagon {% libref(book="1-samuel", chapter=5, verse=3) %}falls before it{% end %} because of an electric discharge, and radioactive materials burn the Philistines. Sendy's *Cahiers* also discusses the ark, but makes it the container of Moses' course notebooks. On {% libref(book="1-kings", chapter=18, verse=38) %}Mount Carmel{% end %}, the fire that consumes {% wiki(slug="elijah") %}Elijah's{% end %} drenched offering is a ray from a craft hidden in the clouds, and Elijah's {% libref(book="2-kings", chapter=2, verse=11) %}ascent{% end %} is a departure as a guest aboard a spaceship. Jonah's "{% libref(book="jonah", chapter=1, verse=17) %}great fish{% end %}" is a submarine in which the creators keep informed of the political events of the time. The {% libref(book="matthew", chapter=13, verse=3) %}parable of the sower{% end %} becomes an account of four attempts to create life on other planets: one destroyed by opponents because it was too close to the creators' world, one lost to a sun too hot, one overgrown on a planet too humid, and one, the Earth, that succeeded.{{ cite(id="2", title="The Book Which Tells the Truth, 3:95–97, 3:111–133, 3:228–233, 4:41–50") }}

There is a structural reason for the gap. Sendy's books rule out the kind of history this commentary describes. In *Ces dieux*, the Celestials leave and their return is « infiniment improbable ». The prophets receive no advice by radio, and no flying saucer lands at the burning bush, because a visitor with such technical superiority would have left unmistakable traces. In *L'ère du Verseau*, Sendy grants that Ezekiel describes something like a flying machine, but concludes that Ezekiel « n'a donc pas vu de cosmonautes ». A reader who followed Sendy closely would have no reason to look for spacecraft in the stories of Elijah or Ezekiel, still less in the Gospels. The Message's commentary from Moses onward depends on its premise of continuing contact, which is exactly the premise Sendy rejected.{{ cite(id="1", title="Ces dieux, chapters 34 and 37: no return, no radio, no visitor at the burning bush") }}{{ cite(id="3", title="L’ère du Verseau, chapter 10: Ezekiel") }}

Two limits apply to this result. First, absence from Sendy is not absence from every earlier writer. Technological readings of {% wiki(slug="ezekiel") %}Ezekiel{% end %} and other prophets were already in circulation by 1973, in {% wiki(slug="erich-von-daniken") %}Erich von Däniken's{% end %} 1968 bestseller and in the French {% wiki(slug="ancient-astronaut-hypothesis") %}ancient-astronaut{% end %} literature. A complete control survey of that literature has not been made, and some of the Message's later readings may have precedents there. Second, the inventory measures how far each author's reading extends and how closely they agree. It does not measure depth. Sendy's treatment of Genesis is much longer and more elaborate than the Message's.

Within those limits, the finding is clear. Sendy's books could have supplied much of the Message's reading of Genesis. They could not have supplied most of the rest, and in several places they argue against it. The Message itself gives an account of where the whole commentary came from: it presents the explanations as given to Vorilhon by the visitor during the six meetings of December 1973, a participant in the history it describes. Within the Raëlian framework, a commentary that runs past the point where Sendy's thesis had to stop is what one would expect from such a source. That remains the framework's explanation. The comparison establishes something narrower: any account of the book's origin that relies on Sendy must also explain the larger part of its biblical commentary, which Sendy does not contain.

## What the skeptical accounts contribute

The skeptical testimonials have made this comparison accessible to readers who might otherwise never encounter Sendy. Several identify concrete parallels and describe reading the older books as a reason to reconsider the Messages' origins. They also repeat one another's examples, so they work best as reading guides whose comparisons still need checking in context.{{ cite(id="7", title="Former Raëlian testimony: Sendy comparison list") }}{{ cite(id="8", title="Additional testimony: comparisons and references to other accounts") }}

One further testimony reports an alleged admission by Raël that he knew Sendy and drank with him in Clermont-Ferrand. The French and English versions of the repost both contain that statement. It is relevant to possible access, but the passage supplies no date for the alleged acquaintance and no independent documentation. Repetition of the report in another testimonial does not turn it into a second witness to the conversation.{{ cite(id="9", title="Additional testimony: alleged acquaintance with Sendy, French and English passages") }}

Sendy supplies an earlier accusation in *Les temps messianiques*. He describes an unnamed young lecturer who has read his books and uses the Elohim name for alleged extraterrestrial pronouncements. Stefano Bigliardi identifies the target as Raël. The passage records Sendy's position, while leaving unexplained how he knew the lecturer's reading history.{{ cite(id="5", title="Les temps messianiques, chapter 17") }}{{ cite(id="6", title="Bigliardi, section 14") }}

Bigliardi also discusses a newspaper report of Sendy's March 1974 lecture in Clermont-Ferrand. The newspaper itself has not been inspected here, and Vorilhon's attendance is unestablished. The lecture also falls after the encounter date reported in the Message, which makes a precise composition and publication chronology essential before assigning it a causal role.{{ cite(id="6", title="Bigliardi, section 13") }}

The wider dispute, including a book-length case naming *L'ère du Verseau* as the primary source and the Raëlian replies to the comparison tables, is surveyed in [*The Man Who Bet the Bible on the Moon*](/articles/the-man-who-bet-the-bible-on-the-moon/). Those works have not been re-examined for this comparison. That article takes Sendy's 1975 remark as showing that the young lecturer had read his books. The present comparison treats Sendy's statement as his claim, since he gives no basis for it, and regards access as not yet established.

These materials give an investigation of influence identifiable leads. They cannot replace the passages themselves, and this article does not use them, or allegations unrelated to the texts, to judge anyone's sincerity.

## How much of a blueprint could Sendy have supplied?

The word *blueprint* can describe different levels of influence. At the broadest level, Sendy offers a way of reading the Bible: recover a plurality of technologically capable beings, treat apparently supernatural acts as material operations, and connect ancient descriptions with modern capacities. At a more specific level, his books contain interpretations that closely resemble those in the Message. At the level of the whole work, the inventory in the previous section shows the limit. Sendy's books can account for most of the Message's reading of Genesis, but very little of its commentary on the rest of the Bible.

The case for selective use is strongest in the cluster of particular readings. Scientific books explain forbidden fruit; exclusion from the sky explains crawling; reduced biological material explains the preservation of species; an attempted ascent explains Babel. These details occur within a broader account of humanlike celestial teachers. An explanation based on independent composition has to account for that conjunction; the general popularity of spaceflight in the period is not enough.

The conjunction still requires weighting. Some elements are connected by the source narrative: Genesis already places prohibited knowledge, the serpent, punishment, the Flood, and Babel in a recognizable sequence. Matching that sequence cannot be counted as if both authors independently invented its order. Likewise, once a writer has adopted technological visitors as a premise, some reinterpretations become easier to generate. The relevant question is which features remain distinctive after those shared resources have been allowed for.

Direct adaptation could account for both the resemblance and the divergence. A writer could retain books as forbidden knowledge while changing the serpent from an experimental subject to a group of sympathetic scientists. A failed attempt to reach the Moon could become a politically threatening mission to another planet. A preserved inheritance could become a continuing dialogue. Those transformations are coherent enough that difference alone offers little protection against a selective-borrowing hypothesis.

The strongest version of the borrowing argument therefore does not require the Message to reproduce Sendy's entire system. It proposes that particular explanations and perhaps some broader philosophical themes were taken up and reorganized around creation, living contact, and a messenger. Evolution, medievalism, and the death of the original visitors could be omitted or replaced because they would obstruct the new account.

That remains a hypothesis about composition. The present comparison has not established a dated reading by Vorilhon before writing, traced distinctive non-biblical phrasing across first editions, or identified shared unusual errors that would make a direct textual relationship difficult to explain away. The testimonies and Sendy's accusation make access worth investigating, but leave those gaps open.

Indirect influence is another possibility. A particular interpretation can circulate through conversation, lectures, translations, or another writer. Under that scenario, the Message might share material with Sendy without having used his books directly. To distinguish it from direct reading, researchers would need actual intermediaries and dates. Naming a broad cultural atmosphere is insufficient unless it can account for the particular interpretations at issue.

Independent use of common sources also remains possible. Both authors read Genesis, and Sendy himself invokes older interpreters and traditions. He also wrote within a crowded French milieu of "fantastic realism" and ancient-astronaut speculation, associated with Louis Pauwels and Jacques Bergier's *Le Matin des magiciens* (1960), Robert Charroux, and, from 1968, Erich von Däniken. Sendy himself mentions « certains amateurs de Mystères » who already linked an atomic destruction of {% wiki(slug="sodom-and-gomorrah") %}Sodom and Gomorrah{% end %} with the Flood. Atomic Sodom, which also appears in the Message, therefore circulated beyond Sendy's own work.{{ cite(id="1", title="Ces dieux, chapter 31: amateurs de Mystères") }}{{ cite(id="2", title="The Book Which Tells the Truth, 2:83") }} A wider comparison could reveal that some supposedly distinctive readings circulated before him. It could equally reveal that their particular combination is rare enough to strengthen the case for his influence. The present article has not conducted that wider survey and cannot assign its result in advance.

A decisive next stage would begin with the earliest French printings. It would align passages without substituting wording from later translations, record additions and deletions, and separate biblical quotations from each author's explanatory prose. It would then examine the history of access and compare plausible alternative sources. That work could narrow the route of transmission much more effectively than increasing the number of loosely described similarities.

For now, selective adaptation has substantial textual grounds as an explanation of the overlap in Genesis. It does not explain the book as a whole. A complete borrowing account would need a second body of sources for the commentary that runs from Moses to the Gospels, and none has yet been identified. Neither direct dependence nor wholly independent composition has been established, and a numerical probability would claim a precision this evidence cannot supply. The balance of the question has nonetheless shifted. "Sendy as blueprint" describes, at most, one chapter of the first Message.

## Two projects worth reading closely

Sendy's distinctive contribution lies in the way he combines natural evolution, celestial intervention, a fragile inherited teaching, and a polemical account of medievalism. His visitors belong to a long biological and cultural history. They leave, die, and continue to matter through descendants and documents. Humanity must recover the capacity to understand what it has received. His speculative method invites objections at many points, but its ambition extends well beyond anticipation of a later religious movement.

The first Message develops a different relationship with the beings of its account. They design terrestrial life, remain able to observe and intervene, explain the past to a living messenger, and ask humanity to prepare for their public arrival. Its biblical interpretations lead towards a commission. Even where a particular explanation closely resembles Sendy's, its place in that relationship can change its meaning.

Read at the level of actual passages, Sendy does not disappear into a list of alleged borrowings, and the Message's distinctive purpose is not mistaken for evidence of independent composition. The historical question stays open, but it is now a narrower one. Several unusual readings of Genesis recur across the two bodies of work in very different arrangements, and what remains to be found is the route by which they travelled, if they travelled at all.
