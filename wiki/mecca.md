**Mecca** (Arabic <span class="arabic">مكة</span> *Makka*; officially
*Makka al-Mukarrama*, "Mecca the Honoured") is a city in the Hijaz region of
western Saudi Arabia and the holiest city of Islam: the site of the **Kaʿba**
within the Sacred Mosque (*al-Masjid al-Ḥarām*), the direction (*qibla*) toward
which Muslims pray five times a day, and the destination of the **hajj**
pilgrimage. The Qur'an names the city *Makka* once (Q 48:24) and its
sanctuary *Bakka* once (Q 3:96), and it attributes the raising of the House to
[Abraham](../abraham/) and his son Ishmael (Q 2:125–127). Islamic tradition
places the birth, the first revelation, and the first preaching of
[Muhammad](../muhammad/) in Mecca, and in its account his entry into the city in
630 CE restored its sanctuary to the worship of the one God.

This entry describes the city in two registers and keeps them apart. The first
is the Islamic tradition's Mecca, told in the tradition's own terms and sources.
The second is a contested revisionist argument, associated with the Canadian
researcher Dan Gibson, that the earliest mosques were oriented toward
[Petra](../petra/) in present-day Jordan. Wheel of Heaven carries that argument
at the strength the evidence allows, which is speculative, and it carries its
principal critic, the historian of Islamic astronomy David A. King, at full
strength. The entry settles the question in neither direction. The Raëlian canon
itself never mentions Mecca.

## Names and etymology

The Arabic name *Makka* appears in the Qur'an in a single verse, which speaks of
God restraining the hands of two hostile parties from one another "in the valley
of Makka" (*bi-baṭni Makka*, Q 48:24). Commentators connect the verse with the
truce of al-Ḥudaybiyya in 628 CE. The form *Bakka* occurs in Q 3:96, which calls
"the first House established for humankind" the one "at Bakka, blessed and a
guidance for the worlds." Classical exegetes read *Bakka* in two main ways:
either as a variant of *Makka*, with the interchange of *b* and *m* known from
other Arabic words, or as the name of the sanctuary precinct within the town.

The Qur'an also uses descriptive titles that the tradition applies to the city.
*Umm al-Qurā*, "the Mother of Towns" (Q 6:92; 42:7), names the centre to which a
warner is sent. *Al-Balad al-Amīn*, "the Secure Land" (Q 95:3), and the House as
"a place of return and security" (Q 2:125) express the city's status as a
sanctuary (*ḥaram*) in which bloodshed is forbidden.

The origin of the name itself is uncertain. For several centuries European
scholars identified Mecca with **Macoraba**, a place listed in Ptolemy's
second-century *Geography* (6.7), and some proposed a derivation from a South
Arabian word for a temple. The historian Ian D. Morris surveyed the history of
this identification in 2018 and concluded that it rests on assumption and that
the pre-Islamic documentary record holds no secure reference to Mecca.

## The Kaʿba and the Sacred Mosque

The **Kaʿba** ("cube") is a stone building of roughly cubic form at the centre of
the Sacred Mosque, covered with a black cloth embroidered in gold, the *kiswa*.
Set in its eastern corner is the **Black Stone** (*al-Ḥajar al-Aswad*), which
pilgrims touch or salute as they begin each circuit around the building. Near
the Kaʿba stands the *Maqām Ibrāhīm*, the "Station of Abraham," which the Qur'an
commands believers to take "as a place of prayer" (Q 2:125). The well of
**Zamzam** lies within the precinct, and a covered gallery joins the low hills
of **al-Ṣafā** and **al-Marwa**.

The Qur'an names the Kaʿba directly as "the Sacred House" which God made "a
standing for the people" (Q 5:97). Muslims do not worship the building. The
Kaʿba is the House of God in the sense of a consecrated place of prayer, and its
function in the daily rite is directional: every mosque in the world is built
with a wall and a niche (*miḥrāb*) indicating the direction of Mecca.

The ninth-century Meccan historian **al-Azraqī** (d. c. 837) preserves in his
*Akhbār Makka* ("Chronicles of Mecca") the most detailed early description of
the sanctuary, including measurements of the Kaʿba as rebuilt in Muhammad's
lifetime. Those measurements give four walls of unequal length. They are the
earliest systematic record of the building and are read by Islamic historians as
the Kaʿba's documented early form. The revisionist argument discussed below reads
them differently.

## Mecca in the Qur'an and the tradition

The Qur'an ties the sanctuary to Abraham. It describes Abraham praying, after
settling some of his offspring "in an uncultivable valley near Your Sacred House,"
that people's hearts would incline toward them (Q 14:37). It records that "Abraham
and Ishmael raised the foundations of the House," praying that God accept the
work and raise up from their descendants a messenger who would recite God's
signs to them (Q 2:127–129). God commands Abraham to purify the House "for those
who circle it, those who stand, and those who bow and prostrate," and to proclaim
the pilgrimage to all people (Q 22:26–27). In the Qur'an's own terms, then, the
House is older than Muhammad's mission, and the faith he preached is the
religion of Abraham (*millat Ibrāhīm*) restored; the [Hanafiyya](../hanafiyya/)
entry treats that self-description.

The ḥadīth literature extends this account with the story of Hagar, Abraham's
wife and Ishmael's mother, who ran seven times between al-Ṣafā and al-Marwa in
search of water for her son until the spring of Zamzam appeared. The pilgrim's
walk between the two hills (*saʿy*) commemorates her search.

The short Sura 106 addresses the tribe of **Quraysh**, the custodians of the
sanctuary in Muhammad's time. It recalls their winter and summer caravan
journeys and calls on them to worship "the Lord of this House." Islamic tradition
describes pre-Islamic Mecca as a trading town and pilgrimage centre whose
sanctuary had filled with idols in the generations after Abraham; it calls the
pre-Islamic age the *jāhiliyya*, the "age of ignorance."

## Muhammad's city

The fullest early narrative of Muhammad's life is the *Sīra* of **Ibn Isḥāq**
(d. c. 767), a Medinan scholar whose work survives chiefly in the recension of
Ibn Hishām (d. 833) and is known in English through Alfred Guillaume's 1955
translation. On its account Muhammad was born in Mecca around 570 CE into the clan
of Hāshim of Quraysh. At about the age of forty, around 610 CE, he received the
first revelation in a cave on Mount Ḥirāʾ outside the city. He preached in Mecca
for roughly twelve years, gathering a small community and meeting growing
opposition from the leaders of Quraysh.

In 622 CE Muhammad and his followers emigrated to Yathrib, afterwards called
Medina. This emigration, the **Hijra**, marks the first year of the Islamic
calendar. Years of conflict with Mecca followed. In 628 CE the Muslims attempted
a pilgrimage, were halted at al-Ḥudaybiyya, and concluded a truce with Quraysh;
in January 630 CE (Ramaḍān 8 AH) Muhammad entered Mecca with an army and the
city surrendered with little fighting. The tradition records that he cleared the
Kaʿba of its idols and granted a general amnesty to the Meccans. In 632 CE he led
the pilgrimage himself, the Farewell Pilgrimage, whose rites became the model
for the hajj, and he died in Medina a few months later.

## The hajj

The **hajj** takes place each year in the month of Dhū al-Ḥijja and is one of the
five pillars of Islam. The Qur'an makes it a duty owed to God by every person
"who is able to find a way to it" (Q 3:97). Its central rites include the
seven circuits of the Kaʿba (*ṭawāf*), the walk between al-Ṣafā and al-Marwa,
the standing at the plain of ʿArafāt, the night at Muzdalifa, the stoning of the
pillars at Minā, and the sacrifice that opens the festival of *ʿĪd al-Aḍḥā*. The
tradition connects several of these rites with Abraham, Hagar, and Ishmael. The
lesser pilgrimage, the *ʿumra*, may be made at any time of year. Non-Muslims may
not enter the city, and its sanctuary has been rebuilt and enlarged repeatedly to
hold pilgrims who now number in the millions.

## The change of qibla

The Qur'an preserves the memory of a change in the direction of prayer. Q 2:142
anticipates that "the foolish among the people will say: what has turned them
from the qibla they used to face?" Q 2:144 answers: "Turn your face toward the
Sacred Mosque; and wherever you are, turn your faces toward it." The surrounding
verses (Q 2:142–150) present the change as a test that distinguishes those who
follow the messenger.

The tradition's account is well defined. In Mecca and for some sixteen or
seventeen months after the Hijra, the Muslims prayed toward Jerusalem; in about
the second year after the Hijra (c. 624 CE) the revelation turned them toward the
Kaʿba. A mosque in Medina, *Masjid al-Qiblatayn* ("the Mosque of the Two
Qiblas"), is remembered as the place where a congregation turned in mid-prayer.
On this account the qibla changed once, from Jerusalem to Mecca, and it has faced
the Kaʿba ever since.

## Historical-critical scholarship

Academic historians of early Islam broadly accept that Islam arose in the Hijaz
and that Mecca was its sanctuary. They also recognise that most written sources
for the city's early history were composed one to two centuries or more after
the events, and they differ over how far those sources can be trusted.

Fred M. Donner, a historian of early Islam at the University of Chicago, argues
in *Muhammad and the Believers* (2010) that Muhammad's movement began as a broad
community of "Believers" whose self-definition as a distinct religion, Islam,
crystallised only toward the end of the seventh century. Donner's reconstruction
keeps the movement's origins in Mecca and Medina. G. R. Hawting, a historian of
early Islam at the School of Oriental and African Studies in London, argues in
*The Idea of Idolatry and the Emergence of Islam* (1999) that the later literary
picture of a Mecca full of idols owes much to polemic, and that the Qur'an's
opponents look more like monotheists judged deficient than like worshippers of
stone images. Each of these works questions part of the received narrative;
none proposes that Islam's sanctuary stood anywhere other than Mecca.

## The early-qibla argument for Petra

A more radical proposal comes from **Dan Gibson**, a Canadian researcher working
outside the academy, in *Qur'anic Geography* (2011) and *Early Islamic Qiblas*
(2017). Gibson surveyed the orientation of mosques built in the first two and a
half centuries of Islam. On his survey the qibla walls of the earliest datable
mosques point toward Petra, the former Nabataean capital more than 1,000 km north of
Mecca, and the Meccan orientation becomes standard only in the second Islamic
century, after a transitional phase.

Walter R. Schumm, a social scientist at Kansas State University, and Zvi
Goldstein analysed Gibson's data and concluded that, under Gibson's assumptions,
the early orientations are consistent with intentional aiming at Petra, with
errors growing with distance from the target. Their analysis rests on Gibson's
measurements and premises, and so it supports the survey from inside; it is not
independent confirmation of it.

{% claim(type="speculative", id="woh-claim-0024") %}On the revisionist reading,
the survey sits within a convergence of further items: Mecca's absence from
Ptolemy's Arabia, the irregular four-sided Kaʿba of al-Azraqī's measurements,
and the tradition's own memory of a change of qibla.{% end %} Each item carries
its own grade, and none is stronger than the survey. The Ptolemy argument is an argument from
silence. Al-Azraqī wrote two centuries after the events, and Gibson's proposed
match between his measurements and an altar at Petra concerns an unexcavated
structure. The qibla-change verses belong to the tradition's primary layer, and
the tradition reads them as a change from Jerusalem. Gibson's fuller
reconstruction, which places the sanctuary's move south in the civil war of
683–692 CE, is the most conjectural part of his case. The [Petra](../petra/) and
[Nabataeans](../nabataeans/) entries and the Explainer
[*The First Mosques Faced Petra, Not Mecca*](/articles/the-first-mosques-faced-petra-not-mecca/)
set out the argument in detail.

## King's critique

The principal scholarly rejection of the Petra hypothesis is by **David A. King**,
historian of science and long-time professor at Goethe University Frankfurt,
whose research on medieval Islamic astronomy and the determination of the qibla
spans several decades. In *From Petra back to Makka* (2019), King grants that
many early mosques do not face Mecca in the strict geographical sense. He denies
that they were therefore aimed at Petra.

King's alternative is the folk astronomy preserved in medieval Islamic texts, a
"sacred geography" in which the world is arranged in sectors around the Kaʿba.
Early builders, on this account, oriented their mosques by alignment to stars,
winds, and the cardinal directions, not by the spherical trigonometry that
computes a geographical bearing. A mosque built on such a scheme faces the Kaʿba
as its builders understood the direction, even when its wall deviates from the
geographical line. On King's account the early orientations reflect a sacred
direction toward the Kaʿba, and the Petra pattern dissolves.

Two further objections stand beside King's. The tradition's own account of a single change,
from Jerusalem to Mecca, may be sufficient to explain the orientation memory
without a second sanctuary. And the convergence assembles items of very unequal
quality: a silence in Ptolemy and descriptions written two centuries later cannot
carry the weight of a relocated holy city.

## In the Wheel of Heaven reading

The Raëlian canon never mentions Mecca, the Kaʿba, Ishmael, or Hagar. Nothing in
the messages refers to the city's sanctuary, its stone, or its pilgrimage, and
this entry draws no reading of them from the canon.

The canon does name the prophet of Islam, in a scene the Explainer
[*The Forty Chairs*](/articles/the-forty-chairs/) examines.
{% claim(type="framework", id="woh-claim-0038") %}In the table scene of the
second message, the speaker points out among the forty or so representatives of
the religions « Mahomet dans les écrits duquel je suis appelé Allah car il
n'osait pas me nommer par respect » ("Mohammed, in whose writings I am called
Allah, for he did not dare to name me out of respect"; ETTMTTP-2:64): one of the
contacted figures through whom, on the canon's account, the Elohim addressed
particular peoples and eras in the register each could carry.{% end %} The corpus
records Mohammed among these figures and does not adjudicate the Islamic
tradition's account of his life, which this entry gives above in the tradition's
own voice.

The canon also speaks of Abraham. In a passage on the people of Israel and
their leader, it reads the sacrifice narrative as a test: the creators tried
Abraham to see whether his feelings toward them were still strong enough
(TBWTT-2:88–89). This is the canon's angle on Abraham, and the corpus reads the
Petra hypothesis alongside it. The passage supplies no geography and says
nothing about a House in Arabia, a son in the desert, or a pilgrimage. It is not
evidence for or against any orientation survey, and no survey is evidence for
it.

{% claim(type="speculative", id="woh-claim-0024") %}Wheel of Heaven records the
Petra reading and the Hijaz tradition side by side and deliberately leaves the
tension between them standing: the corpus registers a convergence and a dispute.
It does not conclude that the sanctuary moved, and it does not set the
hypothesis aside.{% end %} The [Petra](../petra/)
and [Hanafiyya](../hanafiyya/) entries engage the hypothesis; this entry and the
[Muhammad](../muhammad/) entry tell the tradition's account; neither family of
entries is rewritten around the other. The tradition's own texts, the
qibla-change verses among them, are part of the evidence the corpus engages, in
their own terms.
