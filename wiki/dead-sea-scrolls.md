The **Dead Sea Scrolls** are a body of roughly 950 manuscripts recovered
between 1947 and 1956 from eleven caves near Khirbet Qumran, on the northwest
shore of the Dead Sea. Copied between the third century BCE and the first
century CE, they are the oldest surviving witnesses to the text of the
{% wiki(slug="hebrew-bible") %}Hebrew Bible{% end %} — a full millennium older
than the medieval Masoretic codices that had been the earliest complete Hebrew
manuscripts before them — and, alongside the biblical scrolls, they preserve the
rule texts, hymns, and para-biblical literature of a Second Temple Jewish
community that hid its library in jars and never came back for it.

For the Wheel of Heaven framework the scrolls matter twice over. As physical
objects they push the manuscript record of the Hebrew text back to within a few
generations of its authors. As readings, a handful of the variants they preserve
bear directly on the corpus's
{% wiki(slug="plurality-of-gods") %}plurality of gods{% end %} argument — most
sharply a single line of Deuteronomy in which the caves preserve "sons of
{% wiki(slug="elohim") %}Elohim{% end %}" where the later received text was edited
to read "sons of Israel." This entry sets out what the scrolls are, and then
what, on a hedged and auditable reading, they contribute.

## Discovery and the caves

The discovery is told at length in the project's two companion Explainers, and
only its outline belongs here. Some time in the winter that straddled 1946 and
1947, a young Ta'amireh Bedouin threw a stone into a cave opening above the Dead
Sea and heard pottery break. The first cave yielded three scrolls — a complete
book of Isaiah, a rule book for a religious community, and a verse-by-verse
commentary on the prophet Habakkuk. Over the following decade Bedouin
prospectors and archaeologists together worked eleven caves in the cliffs around
the ruin of Qumran, recovering the fragmentary remains of some nine hundred
further manuscripts; Cave 4 alone held the shredded remnants of hundreds of
scrolls.

The project reads the timing through the
{% wiki(slug="apocalypse") %}Apocalypse{% end %} frame the canon supplies — the
caves opened in the first year of the calendar the Raëlian source material counts
from the atomic flash over Hiroshima — but that reading is labelled speculative
where it is made, and nothing in the present entry depends on it. What is not in
dispute is the manuscript evidence itself.

## What the scrolls contain

The library falls into three broad classes. First, the **biblical
manuscripts**: every book of the Hebrew Bible except Esther is represented, some
in multiple copies, the Great Isaiah Scroll (1QIsaᵃ, c. 125 BCE) most complete
among them. Second, the **sectarian texts** — the community's own compositions:
the Community Rule (1QS), the War Scroll (1QM), the Thanksgiving Hymns, and the
*pesharim*, commentaries that read old prophecy as coded dispatches about the
commentator's own last generation. Third, the **para-biblical literature** —
retellings, expansions, and apocalypses that stand outside the later canon:
Jubilees, the Genesis Apocryphon, the Aramaic Enoch literature, and the *Book of
Giants*.

A point the caves settled is that the scriptural canon was not yet closed. Books
the later tradition expelled were copied in bulk: the Enoch literature survives
in roughly eleven Aramaic manuscripts from Cave 4, and Jubilees in around fifteen
copies — several of them, by copy-count, better attested at Qumran than Proverbs.
The community that hid the library had not yet drawn the canonical line the later
synagogue and church would draw.

## The oldest witnesses to the Hebrew text

Before 1947 the earliest complete manuscripts of the Hebrew Bible were the
Masoretic codices of the tenth and eleventh centuries CE — the Aleppo Codex and
the Leningrad Codex. The scrolls pushed that evidence back a thousand years, and
the first lesson they taught was one of stability: the Great Isaiah Scroll matches
the medieval Masoretic Isaiah closely across that entire span, confirming that
the consonantal text was transmitted with high fidelity once it had been fixed.

The second lesson was that, before it was fixed, the text was genuinely fluid.
The caves preserve Hebrew manuscripts agreeing variously with the later Masoretic
Text, with the Hebrew behind the Greek Septuagint, and with the Samaritan
Pentateuch — sometimes side by side, with no sign the community minded. Emanuel
Tov's *Textual Criticism of the Hebrew Bible*, the standard synthesis, treats
this plurality of text-types as the normal condition of scripture in the late
Second Temple period. It is against that background of a still-moving text that
the framework's variants of interest have to be read: they are not later
corruptions of a settled Bible but readings preserved from a time when more than
one wording was in circulation.

## In the Wheel of Heaven framework

Three readings recovered from the caves bear on the corpus's argument, and each
is best stated as a reasonable reading of a real variant rather than as a proof
of anything.

**4QDeutj and the sons of Elohim.** The strongest is a single line of the Song of
Moses. In the received Masoretic text, Deuteronomy 32:8 has the Most High fix the
boundaries of the nations "according to the number of the sons of *Israel*" — a
phrase that has long puzzled readers, since Israel does not yet exist in the
scene. The Qumran fragment 4QDeutj preserves what the verse said before a scribe
corrected it: the nations were divided "according to the number of the sons of
*Elohim*," each people allotted to a member of the divine plurality, with
{% wiki(slug="yahweh") %}Yahweh{% end %} receiving Jacob as his own portion. The
Septuagint's "angels of God" keeps a memory of the same older reading. Mainstream
textual criticism — no ally of this corpus's conclusions — generally judges the
Qumran reading original and the Masoretic wording a theological correction; Tov
calls the change an anti-polytheistic alteration, and Michael Heiser's study of
the verse argues the same case in detail. On the framework's reading this is the
{% wiki(slug="plurality-of-gods") %}plurality{% end %} the corpus argues from,
caught on leather with the edit that removed it visible in the act.

**11QMelchizedek and a heavenly elohim.** The second is a thematic commentary
from Cave 11 about the priest-king Melchizedek, who blesses Abraham in
{% wiki(slug="genesis") %}Genesis{% end %} and then vanishes from the narrative.
The Qumran text makes him a *heavenly* officer who executes judgment at a final
Day of Atonement, and to secure the claim it quotes Psalm 82:1 — "Elohim stands
in the assembly of El, in the midst of elohim he judges" — and identifies the
standing *elohim* as Melchizedek. A community of Torah-observant Judeans, two
centuries before the rabbis, read *elohim* as a term that could designate a member
of a class of powerful beings and applied it to an individual who was neither the
Most High nor a mere metaphor. Whatever one concludes about the framework, the
philological point stands on its own: the singular, abstract "God" is not the only
way the word was heard in antiquity.

**The Enochic material and the Watchers.** The third is the Aramaic Enoch
literature and the *Book of Giants*, both attested at Qumran. Before the caves,
the Book of Enoch survived complete only in Ethiopic, and its antiquity could be
doubted; the eleven Aramaic manuscripts from Cave 4 proved that the
{% wiki(slug="watchers") %}Watchers{% end %} tradition — the descent of the
*benei ha-Elohim* of Genesis 6, their forbidden teaching, and their hybrid
{% wiki(slug="nephilim") %}Nephilim{% end %} offspring — was already scripture in
Judea in the last centuries BCE. The *Book of Giants* goes further still, naming
the Mesopotamian hero Gilgamesh among the giants and binding the
{% wiki(slug="antediluvian") %}antediluvian{% end %} traditions of two cultures
into one narrative. For a corpus that reads the Watchers material as compressed
memory rather than fantasy, the caves supply an early and independent
attestation of the tradition, in its original Aramaic and centuries before the
canon closed.

## What the reading does not claim

The boundary matters, and the entry keeps it explicit. None of these witnesses
proves the framework's conclusions. 4QDeutj establishes that an older Hebrew
reading spoke of a plurality of divine beings; it does not establish that those
beings were a physical creator civilization. 11QMelchizedek shows that ancient
Judeans used *elohim* of a heavenly individual; it does not tell us what they
took that individual to be. The Enochic scrolls prove the antiquity of the
Watchers story, not its historicity. The corpus reads all three as ancient
textual support for the plural-Elohim reading — a convergence between what the
framework argues on other grounds and what the oldest manuscripts happen to
preserve — and it labels that support for what it is: a reasonable reading of
real variants, not a demonstration. Mainstream scholarship accounts for the same
data through the ordinary history of a text that grew more strictly monotheistic
over time, and the framework does not claim that account is wrong so much as
incomplete.

What the scrolls change is the evidentiary situation. The plurality the corpus
reads out of the Hebrew Bible is no longer an inference available only in the
history of translation or in the grammar of the final text; for at least one
load-bearing verse it is written on a manuscript older than the edit that hid it.
That the caves surfaced when they did — into the one generation equipped with the
philology, the photography, and the computing to read and reconstruct them — is
the frame the project's two Explainers,
[*The Scrolls That Woke in Year One*](/articles/the-scrolls-that-woke-in-year-one/)
and [*Unsealed in Year One*](/articles/unsealed-in-year-one/), develop at length,
and to which this entry is the reference companion.
