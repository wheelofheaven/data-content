**Anunnaki** (Sumerian 𒀭𒀀𒉣𒈾, *a-nun-na*) is the collective name, in the
cuneiform traditions of Sumer and Babylonia, for the great gods whose councils
frame the creation of humanity and the decision to send the Flood. In the
Wheel of Heaven framework the Anunnaki are the {% wiki(slug="elohim") %}Elohim{% end %}
remembered under a Mesopotamian name: the same physically embodied
creator-civilization the Hebrew Bible calls the plural *Elohim*, read through
the same {% wiki(slug="neo-euhemerism") %}euhemeristic frame{% end %} that
treats both corpora as compressed records of real makers rather than metaphors
for transcendence.

The identification carries a deliberate boundary. The **identity** of the
beings and the **interpretation** of the surviving records are separate
questions. That the Anunnaki and the Elohim name the same civilization is
explicit in the corpus's canonical frame; which individual names, episodes, and
philological claims follow from that identity is a matter for case-by-case
analysis. In particular, the framework shares the popular Anunnaki–Elohim
identity claim while discarding the apparatus most readers arrive with — the
reconstruction proposed by Zecharia Sitchin.

## What the name means

The philology matters, because much of the popular case rests on a
mistranslation. The Sumerian forms are conventionally normalized *Anuna* or
*Anunna*; Akkadian texts write *Anunnakū*, *Anunnaku*, and the familiar
*Anunnaki*. A divine determinative placed before the name in cuneiform marks
the word as divine but was not pronounced. In standard Assyriology the name
derives from *a-nun-na*, understood as something close to "offspring of the
prince" or "those of princely seed" — often construed as the descendants of
**An**, the sky-god. It is a statement of divine rank and lineage.

Standard lexical analysis does not parse the final syllable of Akkadian
*Anunnaki* as a free-standing Sumerian *ki*, "earth," and the name contains no
verb meaning "came." The widely repeated translation *those who from heaven to
earth came* is therefore a modern interpretive gloss, not a rendering the
source language can bear. The corpus's identification of the Anunnaki with the
Elohim does not depend on it — a point on which Wheel of Heaven parts company
with much ancient-astronaut writing that leans on the false etymology.

## The great gods are not a fixed roster

The Mesopotamian record is internally layered in a way the slogans flatten.
The Anunnaki are not a stable set of names. In older material they are the
senior gods, while a second collective — the **Igigi**, Akkadian *Igīgū*, of
uncertain etymology — performs the manual labor. In
{% libref(book="atrahasis") %}Atra-ḫasīs{% end %} it is the Igigi who revolt
against digging the rivers, and humanity is created precisely to take over that
toil. By the time of {% libref(book="enuma-elish") %}Enūma eliš{% end %} the
Anunnaki are divided by the hundreds between heaven and the underworld, and in
still later texts they drift toward a chthonic, underworld association. The
terms overlap in some compositions and oppose one another in others. Thorkild
Jacobsen, the Danish Assyriologist and historian of Mesopotamian religion,
tracks this drift across periods; any honest comparison has to measure against
a moving target rather than a fixed pantheon.

## Made to bear the labor of the gods

Where the Mesopotamian material converges most sharply with the corpus is not
in a name but in a story: why humanity exists at all. *Atra-ḫasīs* is explicit
that the primeval human, the *lullû*, is formed to carry the *dullu* — the
imposed labor or corvée the poem pictures as a yoke. The lower gods strike; the
wise god **Enki**, called **Ea** in Akkadian, proposes a solution; the mother
goddess mixes clay with the flesh and blood of a slaughtered god; and human
beings are manufactured, in numbers, to carry the labor the gods no longer wish
to carry. *Enūma eliš* gives the same account in a Babylonian key — Ea fashions
humanity from the blood of Qingu "that the gods may be at ease."

That is the same structural claim the corpus reads out of
{% wiki(slug="genesis") %}Genesis{% end %}: humanity as a made thing, fashioned
by a plurality of powerful makers for reasons of their own. The plural
self-address of {% libref(book="genesis-woh", chapter=1, verse=26) %}Genesis 1:26{% end %} —
"let **us** make man in **our** image" — is the Hebrew counterpart to the
Mesopotamian workshop, and the case for reading it as literal plurality is
developed in the {% wiki(slug="plurality-of-gods") %}Plurality of Gods{% end %}
entry. The tablets describe a procedure with a purpose, a method, and a
workforce — closer to an industrial account than to a hymn.

## Enki, the maker who breaks ranks

Enki's role is the sharpest single point of contact. He is the crafty,
humanity-favoring god who, against the council's decision, warns his human
client of the coming Flood — the exact narrative function the corpus assigns to
the dissenting {% wiki(slug="lucifer") %}Lucifer faction{% end %} among the
Elohim, and to the {% wiki(slug="serpent") %}Serpent{% end %} in Eden. The
Sumerologist Samuel Noah Kramer's studies of the Enki myths describe a god who
takes humanity's side against his own council. Wheel of Heaven reads that
recurring figure — the maker who breaks ranks to protect the made — as one of
the load-bearing convergences between the traditions, and the council-decreed
deluge itself is treated in the {% wiki(slug="great-flood") %}Great Flood{% end %}
entry.

The wider comparative field preserves the pattern without sharing the names:
the Greek Prometheus steals fire and the arts for humanity against Zeus's will;
the Indian Manu is warned before a world-destroying deluge. These are useful
comparisons of narrative function — the divine dissenter, the warning, the
preserved human line — but resemblance alone does not make Prometheus, Manu,
Enki, and Lucifer one individual. The Enki–Lucifer connection is a canonical
reading; the wider parallels describe a recurring mythic type.

## In the Wheel of Heaven framework

At the level of the corpus's canonical frame the verdict is direct: **the
Anunnaki are the Elohim**, the same creator-civilization remembered under a
Mesopotamian name. *Anunnaki* is registered among the alternative names of the
{% wiki(slug="elohim") %}Elohim{% end %} not as a loose typological resemblance
but as an identification of the underlying beings, while the two traditions
preserve different names, emphases, and narrative layers. What the cuneiform
record supplies is a structural series that matches the biblical one — plural
makers, the fashioning of humanity, labor assigned to the made, conflict within
the {% wiki(slug="council-of-the-eternals") %}council{% end %}, a council-decreed
Flood, and a dissenter who preserves a human line.

The identity is stated as framework, not as established history. The cuneiform
and Hebrew records do not use the same vocabulary, and the tablets alone do not
prove that the two traditions remember one civilization; they establish that
the *pattern* is shared. Wheel of Heaven reads that shared pattern as the
Mesopotamian memory of the Elohim. The identity is canonical; the philological
and comparative steps remain auditable.

## What the framework does not claim

The identification is not Zecharia Sitchin's argument. His *Earth Chronicles*
proposed a specific and testable apparatus: the Anunnaki come from **Nibiru**,
a planet on a 3,600-year orbit; they came to mine gold; they engineered
humanity by splicing their genes into *Homo erectus*; and essentially every
ancient pantheon names the same beings. The corpus adopts none of it. In
Akkadian, *nibiru* denotes a crossing or a marker-star — in *Enūma eliš*
prominently Jupiter — not a hidden planet on a long orbit; there is no such body
in the solar system. The 3,600-year orbit is Sitchin's own construction, and
his translations are rejected across Assyriology. Collective identity likewise
does not license merging every Sumerian, Egyptian, Greek, and Vedic figure into
a single individual: the Anunnaki and the Elohim can name the same civilization
while individual mappings remain comparative claims to be argued one by one.
