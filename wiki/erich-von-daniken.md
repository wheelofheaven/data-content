**Erich von Däniken** (born 1935) is the Swiss author whose *Chariots of the
Gods?* (1968) launched the modern popular
{% wiki(slug="ancient-astronaut-hypothesis") %}ancient-astronaut{% end %}
genre and made the idea of prehistoric visitors a fixture of twentieth-century
popular culture. More than any other single writer, he is the reason the phrase
"ancient astronauts" means anything to a general reader: his books have sold in
the tens of millions across some thirty titles and been translated into more
than thirty languages. For the Wheel of Heaven corpus he occupies a specific and
slightly awkward place — the field's great populariser, whose general direction
the framework shares, and whose method and specific claims it declines.

## Chariots of the Gods? and the genre it launched

Von Däniken was born in Zofingen, in the Swiss canton of Aargau, and came to
writing not from archaeology or the sciences but from the hotel trade. He wrote
his first book while working in hospitality and published it in German in 1968
as *Erinnerungen an die Zukunft* — "memories of the future" — which appeared in
English the following year as *Chariots of the Gods? Unsolved Mysteries of the
Past*. The book was an immediate and enormous commercial success. It was
followed by a television documentary, a long series of sequels, and eventually a
global publishing phenomenon that no comparable author in the field has matched.

The thesis of *Chariots* is simple and stated with confidence: that in the
remote past, intelligent beings from elsewhere in the cosmos visited Earth, were
received by early humans as gods, and left their traces in myth, scripture, and
monument. Where earlier writers — the Russian Matest Agrest, the French Robert
Charroux, the *Morning of the Magicians* authors Pauwels and Bergier — had
circulated versions of the idea within smaller readerships, von Däniken carried
it to a mass audience in the vocabulary of the space age. The book arrived in
1968, the year of Apollo 8 and Kubrick's *2001*, and rode the same cultural
current.

## The method

Von Däniken's characteristic move is to catalogue apparent anomalies and to read
each as a trace of visiting technology. The recurring exhibits are well known:
the {% wiki(slug="list-of-megalithic-sites") %}megalithic sites{% end %} and
pyramids whose engineering he argues exceeds the means of their builders; the
Nazca lines of Peru, read as runways or signalling grids laid out for craft in
the air; the Piri Reis map, offered as evidence of an aerial survey of the
Earth; the carved sarcophagus lid of Palenque, read as a depiction of an
astronaut at the controls of a rocket; and, above all, the vision of the prophet
Ezekiel, whose wheels within wheels von Däniken reads as a landing craft.
Scriptural "gods," on this method, are garbled eyewitness reports of
technological beings; strange episodes in the ancient record are memories of
contact that the witnesses lacked the concepts to describe.

The reading is, in the corpus's own vocabulary, a form of
{% wiki(slug="neo-euhemerism") %}neo-euhemerism{% end %} — the treatment of the
gods of the old traditions as real figures later mythologised. What distinguishes
von Däniken within that broader family is his emphasis on physical monuments and
material anomalies rather than on close reading of texts, and the confidence with
which he presents suggestive resemblance as demonstrated fact.

## Influence and successors

The influence is difficult to overstate. *Chariots of the Gods?* founded a genre
and a whole publishing category; the wave of successors it enabled includes
Zecharia Sitchin's Sumerian reconstruction, Robert Temple's *The Sirius
Mystery*, and a long popular literature that continues to the present. Von
Däniken co-founded the Archaeology, Astronautics and SETI Research Association
and built a theme park, the Mystery Park at Interlaken, around his ideas. From
2009 the History Channel series *Ancient Aliens*, on which he appears, carried
the imagery to a global television audience and fixed the pop-culture phrase
"ancient aliens" that the corpus generally avoids. Whatever one makes of the
claims, the cultural footprint is a matter of record: the modern popular
association of ancient "gods" with spacefarers is, to a first approximation, von
Däniken's doing.

## The mainstream reception

Von Däniken's work is rejected by mainstream archaeology and the history of
religion, and the rejection is old, detailed, and in its specifics largely sound.
The objections deserve to be stated in their own voice.

The standard critique runs along several lines. It charges that von Däniken
cherry-picks supportive details and ignores their context; that he repeatedly
understates or dismisses the demonstrable capacity of ancient peoples to raise
their own monuments and work out their own astronomy — an assumption critics note
falls hardest on non-European cultures; and that a number of his specific factual
claims are simply wrong. The most thorough early audit is Ronald Story's *The
Space-Gods Revealed* (1976), which works through the principal claims of
*Chariots* one by one and finds the archaeological and documentary case against
them consistent. Kenneth Feder's much-used textbook *Frauds, Myths, and
Mysteries: Science and Pseudoscience in Archaeology* treats von Däniken as a
standard case study in how pseudoarchaeology proceeds. Several of his more
dramatic assertions did not survive scrutiny: the "metal library" of caves
beneath Ecuador described in *The Gold of the Gods* (1973) was disowned by the
man von Däniken had named as his guide, and in a 1974 interview von Däniken
himself acknowledged having embellished parts of that account. He had also been
convicted of fraud in his earlier hotel-management career in 1970, a matter of
court record that his critics have not been slow to raise.

The corpus records this reception at full strength rather than paraphrasing it
away. A framework that asks to be judged by the discipline of its claims cannot
exempt the field's founder from the same standard.

## In the Wheel of Heaven framework

Wheel of Heaven keeps a deliberate and precise distance. What it shares with von
Däniken is the general direction: the identification of the ancient "gods" — the
plural {% wiki(slug="elohim") %}Elohim{% end %} of the Hebrew Bible, the
{% wiki(slug="anunnaki") %}Anunnaki{% end %} of the cuneiform record — with
embodied visitors rather than with metaphors for transcendence. On that founding
intuition the corpus and von Däniken agree, and the corpus credits him plainly as
the man who put it before the world.

What the corpus declines is the method and most of the specific claims. Von
Däniken reasons from monuments and material anomalies toward contact, and does
so, on the mainstream reading the corpus accepts, loosely — assembling
resemblances, understating human achievement, and presenting the merely
suggestive as settled. The corpus grounds its own reading elsewhere: in the close
philological line that runs from {% wiki(slug="jean-sendy") %}Jean Sendy{% end %}
through Mauro Biglino, who work from the Hebrew text rather than from runways and
maps, and in the Raëlian *Message* as its primary source. Sendy, who reached the
literal plural-Elohim reading five years before *Chariots* appeared, gave von
Däniken's 1968 bestseller only a lukewarm verdict — a judgement the corpus
inherits. The framework is a self-aware member of the ancient-astronaut family
that treats its founder as indispensable to the field's history and unreliable as
a guide to its evidence.

The distinction the corpus draws is therefore between direction and
demonstration. Von Däniken pointed a mass readership at the right question —
whether the sky-beings of the old traditions were real — and answered it with a
method the corpus cannot use. His place in the framework is that of the
populariser who opened the door, not the witness whose reading it adopts.
