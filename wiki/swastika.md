The **swastika** is one of the oldest religious symbols, with archaeological evidence extending back at least 12,000 years to Paleolithic Europe. It is a **cross with four arms bent at right angles**, typically rotated 90 degrees between successive arms, in a right-facing (clockwise, *svastika* proper in Hindu tradition) or left-facing (counter-clockwise, *sauvastika*) orientation. Its cross-cultural reach spans most major traditions: it is a principal auspicious symbol in Hindu tradition (Sanskrit *svastika*, "well-being" or "good fortune"); appears in nearly all Buddhist temples, notably on the Buddhapada (Buddha's footprint) and in the Chinese 卍 (*manji*) form; is one of the eight auspicious symbols (*ashtamangala*) in Jain tradition; and had pre-Nazi use across European, Mesopotamian, Mediterranean, Native American, and other traditions. Its meaning varies by tradition but consistently carries auspicious, protective, and cosmic-cyclic content.

Within the Wheel of Heaven framework, the swastika is the **central element of the original Raëlian Symbol of Infinity** — the form Yahweh delivered to Raël at the December 13, 1973 contact at Puy-de-Lassolas, engraved on the alliance craft and on Yahweh's suit. Its Raëlian meaning is the **temporal dimension of infinity** through the cyclic-eternal principle. Yahweh's gloss: *"the swastika, which means that everything is cyclic, the top becoming the bottom, and the bottom in turn becoming the top."* A later passage extends it: *"That is why we can find the swastika in our symbol, as in numerous ancient writings, which signifies 'the cycle'. It is the choice between paradise, which the peaceful use of science makes possible, and the hell of returning to the primitive stage."* The original form remains in use in Asian Raëlian branches; in 1990, at Raël's suggestion, the Elohim authorized replacing the central element with a galaxy-shaped spiral for Western branches, in response to the post-Holocaust complications around the symbol.

The Nazi appropriation (1920-1945) — with the tilted-clockwise *Hakenkreuz* adopted as the NSDAP symbol in 1920 and the German national flag from 1933-1945 — produced the post-1945 complications that shape the symbol's contemporary Western reception. Various European jurisdictions restrict its display, with exceptions for religious and educational use, while reception in post-Holocaust Europe and North America remains fraught. Post-1945 reclamation efforts — within Hindu-Buddhist diaspora communities, the World Hindu Council, and scholarly and journalistic work (notably Mukti Jain Campion's 2014 BBC documentary *The Swastika: A Symbol Beyond Redemption?*) — have sought to distinguish the pre-Nazi religious usage from the Nazi appropriation. This entry treats the swastika as a symbol in its cross-cultural and historical context; the composite emblem in which it sits is covered in the [Raëlian Symbol of Infinity](../raelian-symbol-of-infinity/) entry.

## Etymology and naming

### Sanskrit "svastika" as etymological anchor

The Sanskrit **svastika** (सुस्तिक; also *swastika* or *suastika*) anchors the symbol's modern international name. It combines:

- **sv-** (सु-): "good," "well," "auspicious"
- **asti** (अस्ति): "is" / "exists," from the verb *as-* (to be)
- **-ka** (-क): a nominal suffix

*Sv-asti-ka* thus reads as "that which is good" or "the auspicious." The term was adopted into French, English, German, and other European scholarship in the late nineteenth century and has anchored international usage since.

### Cross-cultural designations

- **Hindi/Sanskrit**: *svastika* (सुस्तिक) — the principal designation
- **Chinese**: 卍 (*wàn*) or 萬字 (*wànzì*, "ten-thousand character"), both religious symbol and written character
- **Japanese**: 卍 (*manji*)
- **Korean**: 만 (*man*)
- **Tibetan**: གཡུང་དྲུང་ (*g.yung-drung*, "eternal," "permanent")
- **Greek**: τετρασκελής (*tetraskelis*) or γαμμάδιον (*gammadion*, from four gammas)
- **Latin**: *crux gammata* (again, four gammas)
- **Old Norse**: *fylfot* ("four-foot")
- **Basque**: *lauburu* ("four-head")
- **Finnish**: *hakaristi* ("hook-cross")
- **Latvian**: *ugunskrusts* ("fire-cross") and *pērkonkrusts* ("thunder-cross")
- **Lithuanian**: *swastika* and *šuolinis kryžius*
- **Armenian**: *arevakhach* (արևախաչ, "sun-cross")
- **Georgian**: *borjgali* (ბორჯღალი)
- **Sami**: *mursunsydän* ("walrus-heart")

### "Hakenkreuz" — the Nazi-specific designation

The German **Hakenkreuz** ("hook-cross") emerged in the late nineteenth century. The Nazi regime used it for the tilted-clockwise form (rotated 45 degrees from the horizontal-vertical axis) in NSDAP and state contexts, and post-1945 German usage retains the term chiefly for that Nazi-specific form.

### Post-1945 designation conventions

Post-1945 scholarship has variously distinguished the pre-Nazi symbol from the Nazi appropriation:

- many distinguish **swastika** (the broader symbol) from **Hakenkreuz** or **Nazi swastika**
- some reserve **manji** for Buddhist usage, **svastika** for Hindu usage, and **swastika** for the broader Western-language term
- Hindu-Buddhist diaspora communities often advocate the regional names (svastika, manji) precisely to register the distinction

The Wheel of Heaven corpus uses **swastika** as its principal designation, deploying the regional names where they are operationally clearer, and works within these post-1945 conventions.

## Conventional understanding

### The archaeological record

Systematic scholarship on the swastika dates from the late nineteenth century. **Thomas Wilson**'s *The Swastika: The Earliest Known Symbol, and Its Migrations; with Observations on the Migration of Certain Industries in Prehistoric Times* (Smithsonian Institution Annual Report, 1894) is the foundational treatment. Wilson found the swastika to be one of the oldest religious symbols, with evidence going back 10,000-12,000 years; distributed across nearly all major traditions; varying in meaning but consistently auspicious, protective, or cosmic-cyclic; and anchored etymologically in the Sanskrit *svastika*. Later archaeology (Joseph Campbell's comparative mythology, Marija Gimbutas on Neolithic European symbolism, and others) confirmed and extended these findings.

### Cross-cultural meanings

Comparative-religion scholarship records consistent patterns beneath the variation:

- **Auspiciousness and good fortune** — the Hindu, Buddhist, and Jain baseline: the Sanskrit *svastika* (well-being), Buddhist use on the Buddhapada and in temple iconography, Jain inclusion among the *ashtamangala*.
- **Cyclic cosmic time** — especially in Buddhist and broader Asian traditions; the four arms rotating about a center render cyclic-rotational content directly.
- **Solar associations** — in Indo-European and European traditions; the "sun-cross" names (Armenian *arevakhach*, others) register this.
- **Protective / apotropaic use** — in domestic, ritual, and broader contexts.

## The concept's content

### Geometric forms

**Right-facing (clockwise)**. The classical *svastika*: arms bend clockwise (upper arm to the right, right arm down, lower arm to the left, left arm up). Hindu tradition ties it to Vishnu, the day, the male principle, and the sun. The Nazi *Hakenkreuz* uses this orientation.

**Left-facing (counter-clockwise)**. The *sauvastika*, the mirror image; Hindu tradition ties it to Kali, the night, the female principle, and the moon. It is common in Buddhist temple contexts.

**Tilted forms**. The Nazi *Hakenkreuz* specifically used a 45-degree tilt, turning the cross into an X- rather than +-shape — one feature distinguishing it from most traditional usages.

**Variant forms**. The Greek meander, the Chinese 卍 (*manji*), medallion integrations, the Navajo "whirling logs," and other regional variations. The central swastika of the original Raëlian Symbol of Infinity draws on the Asian-Buddhist form, not the Nazi one.

## Modern reinterpretations

### The nineteenth-century "Aryan" appropriation

Late-nineteenth-century European scholarship set up the conditions for the Nazi appropriation.

**Friedrich Max Müller** (1823-1900), whose *Lectures on the Science of Language* (1861-1864) framed the era's understanding of Indo-European links, popularized the term **"Aryan"** (from Sanskrit *ārya*, originally a self-designation of the Indo-Iranian-language-speaking peoples). The term was increasingly conflated with racial-political identity, with grave later consequences; Müller himself, in his later work, explicitly cautioned against conflating linguistic and racial categories, but the conflation continued.

**Heinrich Schliemann** (1822-1890) found many swastika artifacts at Troy in the 1870s; his *Trojanische Alterthümer* (1875) popularized the symbol in European archaeology, and his identification of it as "Aryan" fed the later nationalist appropriation.

**The Theosophical Society** (founded 1875 by Helena Blavatsky and Henry Steel Olcott) placed the swastika in its seal alongside the Star of David, the Ouroboros, the Ankh, and the Aum. Blavatsky's *The Secret Doctrine* (1888) read the swastika as "the continual motion and revolution of the invisible Kosmos of Forces… the rotation in the cycles of Time of the world's axes," adding that "the cross is equivalent to the circle of the year" — a reading the corpus files as reception history, but one that anticipates almost exactly the cyclic-time content the framework reads in the symbol.

Together with European nationalist-romantic currents (in Germany, Austria, and Scandinavia), these channels carried the swastika into early-twentieth-century politics.

### The Nazi appropriation (1920-1945)

**Adolf Hitler**, in *Mein Kampf* (1925), describes the NSDAP symbol: the tilted-clockwise *Hakenkreuz* (rotated 45 degrees), black on a white circle within a red field. Adopted as the party symbol in 1920, it became, after the 1933 accession, the symbol of the German state — on the national flag (1935-1945), military uniforms, government buildings, and official documents. Its Nazi-era uses ran from the NSDAP party flag and national flag through Wehrmacht and SS iconography (with variations including the SS-runes), the Hitler Youth, propaganda materials, and concentration-camp contexts. Twelve years of state use bound the symbol to the regime's actions — the Holocaust and the systematic violence against Jews, Roma, disabled persons, political opponents, and other targeted groups.

### Post-1945 legal frameworks

Post-Holocaust law restricts the symbol's display. In Germany, **Strafgesetzbuch §86** ("Dissemination of propaganda materials of unconstitutional and terrorist organizations") and **§86a** ("Use of symbols of unconstitutional and terrorist organizations") prohibit Nazi symbols including the *Hakenkreuz*, with exceptions for educational, scholarly, journalistic, and counter-propaganda use. Austria's *Verbotsgesetz 1947* does likewise, and France, Hungary, the Czech Republic, Slovakia, Poland, and other jurisdictions have their own provisions. These frameworks generally distinguish the Nazi *Hakenkreuz* from the broader swastika, exempting religious and educational contexts. In North America, the US First Amendment and Canadian religious-freedom protections preserve display rights, though reception remains largely negative absent legal prohibition.

### Post-1945 reclamation efforts

Efforts to separate the pre-Nazi religious usage from the Nazi appropriation include public-education campaigns, formal statements from Hindu and Buddhist organizations, and legal-political engagement by diaspora communities. **The World Hindu Council (Vishva Hindu Parishad)** has pressed the case across the period. **Mukti Jain Campion's** BBC documentary *The Swastika: A Symbol Beyond Redemption?* (2014) became a prominent articulation of the reclamation discourse, alongside book-length treatments — **Steven Heller's** *The Swastika: Symbol Beyond Redemption?* (Allworth Press, 2000) and **Malcolm Quinn's** *The Swastika: Constructing the Symbol* (Routledge, 1994). The situation stays split: the pre-Nazi content thrives in Hindu-Buddhist-Jain diaspora communities, while Western reception remains shaped by the Nazi legacy.

## Comparative observations

The swastika's presence across nearly all major traditions warrants comparative attention.

### Hindu svastika

The Hindu tradition supplies the etymology and much of the symbol's religious foundation. The Sanskrit *svastika* (सुस्तिक), from *sv-* (good, well) and *asti* (to be) plus *-ka*, means "well-being," "good fortune," "auspiciousness." The symbol predates the Vedic period: it appears in Indus Valley artifacts (c. 3,300-1,300 BCE) at Harappa, Mohenjo-daro, and other sites, suggesting depth beyond the Indo-Aryan migration. Vedic and post-Vedic texts carry it as a principal auspicious sign, and classical Hindu practice uses it widely — temple architecture, household markings, Diwali rangoli, weddings, and Vishnu iconography. Hindu tradition distinguishes the right-facing *svastika* (Vishnu, day, male principle) from the left-facing *sauvastika* (Kali, night, female principle), and the symbol remains in extensive contemporary use, untouched by the Western complications.

### Buddhist usage

Buddhist tradition offers one of the fullest articulations. The swastika appears on the **Buddhapada** (Buddha's footprint), typically at the heel or center. The Chinese Buddhist **卍 (*manji*)** is both symbol and written character, carrying "ten thousand years," "infinity," "eternity" (encoded as Unicode U+5350 for left-facing 卐 and U+534D for right-facing 卍). Japanese Buddhism uses both forms, and Japanese maps mark temples with the symbol; a 2016 government proposal to drop it from maps for visitors to the 2020 Tokyo Olympics was withdrawn after domestic opposition citing its religious meaning. Tibetan tradition uses it as *g.yung-drung* (གཡུང་དྲུང་, "eternal"), and the Bardo Thodol (Tibetan Book of the Dead, eighth century, Padmasambhava and Yeshe Tsogyal) — which the source cross-references — carries it. The post-1945 Asian context preserves this usage without the Western complications, and the symbol still appears in nearly all Buddhist temples.

### Jain ashtamangala usage

In Jain tradition the swastika is one of the eight auspicious symbols (*ashtamangala*), alongside the *Shrivatsa*, *Nandavarta*, *Vardhmanaka*, *Bhadrasana*, *Kalasha*, *Minayugma*, and *Darpana*. It represents the seventh Tirthankara, **Suparshva**, and its four arms figure the four states of existence in Jain cosmology — heavenly beings (*deva*), humans (*manushya*), animals (*tiryancha*), and hellish beings (*naraka*) — mapping the symbol's rotation onto the rebirth cycle. Jains draw it in rice at the start of ceremonies.

### Pre-Nazi European traditions

**Mezin culture (Ukraine, c. 10,000 BCE)**. A carved-mammoth-ivory bracelet from the Mezin site preserves the earliest claimed swastika. The reading is contested — the pattern is a continuous diagonal meander only resembling linked swastikas, and its Paleolithic dating is broad — so the safer statement is the one the United States Holocaust Memorial Museum uses: the motif appears across Eurasia *"as early as 7,000 years ago, perhaps representing the movement of the sun through the sky,"* and remains auspicious in Hinduism, Buddhism, and Jainism to the present.

**Cucuteni-Trypillian culture (Ukraine-Romania-Moldova, c. 5,000-3,000 BCE)** and **Vinča culture (Serbia, c. 5,500-4,500 BCE)** preserve swastika usage on pottery and ritual objects. **Greek geometric pottery** (c. 900-700 BCE) features it heavily, giving the Greek *gammadion* (four gammas). **Etruscan** (c. 800-100 BCE) and **Roman** usage follows. Various **Celtic, Germanic, Slavic, and Baltic** traditions carry it — Germanic with Thor's-hammer associations in some readings, Latvian as *ugunskrusts* (fire-cross) and *pērkonkrusts* (thunder-cross), tied to the thunder-god Pērkons (parallel to the Indo-European thunder-god tradition). Together these register substantial pre-Nazi European usage across distinct cultures.

### Native American and indigenous variants

The **Navajo** used the swastika as a sacred symbol in the *whirling logs* tradition, including in healing sand-paintings. In 1940, in response to the Nazi appropriation, the Navajo Nation formally renounced it: *"Because the above ornament which has been a symbol of friendship among our forefathers for many centuries has been desecrated recently by another nation of peoples, therefore, it is resolved that henceforth from this date on and forever more our tribes renounce the use of the emblem commonly known today as the swastika..."* The **Hopi** used both forms in ceremony, and other Native American traditions preserve parallel usage.

### Mesopotamian, Mediterranean, and Egyptian usage

Various Mesopotamian traditions carry the swastika across long spans, as do the ancient Mediterranean cultures treated above (Greek, Etruscan, Roman). Ancient Egypt used it only marginally, working mostly through other symbolic frameworks.

### The convergence

The corpus reads the swastika's global reach — across nearly all major traditions, with 12,000+ years of archaeological depth and consistent meaning (auspiciousness, cyclic cosmic time, well-being, protection) — as evidence of a broader pattern. It judges the mainstream explanation (independent development plus cultural diffusion) insufficient to account for the breadth and the specific structural parallels: the recurring links to auspiciousness, to cyclic content, and to the same geometric form across widely separated traditions. On the framework's reading, that shared inheritance preserves memory of the alliance's operational presence, each tradition retaining the symbol in its own idiom — grounded in the Yahweh-attributed claim that *"we can find the swastika in our symbol, as in numerous ancient writings, which signifies 'the cycle'."*

The framework does not reject the mainstream picture wholesale: diffusion certainly occurred, and the traditions show both common structure and local elaboration. What it adds is the underlying situation the mainstream account, lacking the corpus's cosmology, has had to attribute to independent development and diffusion alone. The Nazi appropriation (1920-1945) stands as one twelve-year aberration within this longer context — producing lasting post-Holocaust complications without erasing the 12,000-year tradition — and the 1990 modification answers those complications while keeping the pre-Nazi content intact in non-Western use.
