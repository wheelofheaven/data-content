**PURSUE** — the **Presidential Unsealing and Reporting System for UAP
Encounters** — is the United States government's program for finding,
declassifying, and publicly releasing unresolved records on **unidentified
anomalous phenomena (UAP)**. Ordered by a presidential directive in February
2026 and administered by the Department of War with support from the Office of
the Director of National Intelligence, it publishes tranches of documents,
images, videos, and audio recordings at a single public portal, war.gov/UFO,
on a rolling cadence — five releases between May 8 and August 7, 2026, with
further releases announced as actively in work. The Department describes the
undertaking as requiring "coordination between dozens of agencies and the
review of tens of millions of records, many existing only on paper."

Two things distinguish PURSUE from every prior U.S. government engagement with
the subject. It is **public by default** — records go to an open website with
no security clearance and no Freedom of Information Act request required — and
it is **government-wide**, drawing not only on military and intelligence
holdings but, by its fifth release, on the files of the Department of Energy,
the State Department, and the Executive Office of the President itself. Every
item is catalogued as an *unresolved case*: the program publishes what the
government could not identify, and it publishes its negatives too.

The Wheel of Heaven framework tracks PURSUE closely — the corpus files a
[dispatch](/news/pursue-release-05-triangles-and-the-white-house-file/) on
each release — but under a deliberate discipline: registration without
enlistment. No file PURSUE releases is evidence for the framework, and no case
it debunks is evidence against it. The framework's account of contact rests on
the canon's own [reports](../rael/), not on any craft in any sky.

## The name and the mandate

The program's full name styles it as *presidential* — an unsealing carried out
under the direct authority of the president rather than as an agency
initiative. Its published mandate is to "expeditiously find, review, identify,
declassify and publicly release unresolved UAP-related records and historical
documents in the federal government's possession," with new material posted
"on a rolling basis as files are reviewed and declassified." Secretary of War
Pete Hegseth characterized the department as "in lockstep with President Trump
to bring unprecedented transparency regarding our government's understanding
of Unidentified Anomalous Phenomena."

The mandate's key word is *unresolved*. PURSUE does not publish the
government's identified cases (the aircraft, balloons, and sensor artifacts
that make up the bulk of any UAP dataset), and it does not adjudicate the
cases it publishes. It is an archive-opening program, not an analysis program
— analysis remains with the All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office (AARO), the
Pentagon's standing UAP office, to which the PURSUE portal itself directs
readers for the wider historical record.

## Origin: the statute before the directive

PURSUE's founding instrument on the public record is a **presidential
directive of February 19, 2026**, in which President Donald Trump ordered
federal agencies to identify, review, and declassify their UAP-related
holdings. Early press coverage widely described the program as created by an
executive order; no numbered executive order has been published, and the
Department of War's own materials speak only of the president's directive.
The Department stood the program up within weeks — legal analysis dates the
task force to May 1, 2026 — and published the first tranche at war.gov/UFO on
May 8.

The directive did not arrive on empty ground. Congress had already built the
legal machinery in the FY2023 National Defense Authorization Act, enacted in
December 2022, which created a statutory channel — codified at **50 U.S.C.
§ 3373b** — for current and former government personnel to make authorized
disclosures of UAP-related information notwithstanding any nondisclosure
agreement, and prohibited reprisal against those who use it. PURSUE is, in
structural terms, an executive push through a door Congress had opened four
years earlier.

The two layers joined on **July 31, 2026**, when the Office of the Director of
National Intelligence issued preliminary implementing guidance (ES
2026-00818): agencies were directed to designate PURSUE representatives within
thirty days, to establish processes for their workforces to disclose UAP
information, and to inform employees that nondisclosure agreements purporting
to prevent such disclosures are "no longer in force." The guidance's
application to government *contractors* — the population at the center of the
2023 congressional testimony about alleged retrieval programs — was left to
follow-on guidance, and remains the program's most closely watched open edge.

## How the program operates

The portal at war.gov/UFO presents a searchable database — filterable by
agency, release, incident date, incident location, and file type — that stood
at **375 files across the five releases** as of late August 2026. Files carry
structured identifiers by contributing agency and type (`DOW-UAP-D101`,
`FBI-UAP-D024`, `EOP-UAP-D001`), and tranches have arrived on an
approximately monthly rhythm: two, three, and four-week intervals settling at
four weeks. Public interest has been considerable; the Pentagon's chief
spokesman put portal traffic at over 1.7 billion hits within the program's
first five weeks.

The releases mix sensor material (gun-camera and infrared footage, radar-scope
recordings), witness reporting (FBI interview records, with digital renderings
the Bureau produces to illustrate testimony — illustrations, the corpus's
dispatches note, not photographs), and archival holdings reaching back to a
1947 intelligence review of the Scandinavian "ghost rockets." Notably, the
program releases **negative findings** alongside its unknowns: its fifth
tranche included a 1963 case — a Brazilian radio report of a crashed sphere,
inquired after at the White House's space council — that the released State
Department cables themselves resolve as a press fabrication. An archive that
publishes its own debunkings is behaving like an archive rather than a press
office, and the corpus registers that in the program's favor.

One practical asymmetry is worth recording: the portal is open to human
readers but closed to machines — its content-delivery configuration blocks
automated retrieval outright, so mirrors, archives, and researchers work from
secondary captures rather than the live site.

## Institutional precedents

PURSUE is the latest instrument in a documented seventy-year sequence, and is
best understood against it.

The U.S. Air Force ran three successive investigations — **Project Sign**
(1948), **Project Grudge** (1949), and **Project Blue Book** (1952–1969) —
that together examined 12,618 sightings and left 701 formally unidentified.
Blue Book was closed on the recommendation of the University of Colorado's
**Condon Report** (1968), whose conclusion that further study offered no
scientific payoff set the government's public posture for nearly five
decades. What followed was the FOIA era: declassification by adversarial
request, piecemeal and slow.

The modern sequence began when the *New York Times* revealed the **Advanced
Aerospace Threat Identification Program** (AATIP, 2007–2012) in December
2017. The Navy's UAP Task Force followed in 2020, ODNI's preliminary
assessment (144 military reports, most unresolved) in 2021, and the
establishment of **AARO** in 2022. In July 2023, congressional testimony —
most prominently from former intelligence officer David Grusch — alleged
undisclosed craft-retrieval and reverse-engineering programs; AARO's 2024
*Historical Record Report* answered that it had found "no verifiable
evidence" any U.S. government program has ever possessed extraterrestrial
technology. That report is the government's own skeptical instrument, and the
corpus carries it at documentary strength.

Against that lineage, PURSUE's novelty is structural rather than substantive:
publication by default instead of by request; a rolling cadence instead of
episodic releases; a single consolidated portal instead of scattered reading
rooms; and a contributing roster that reaches beyond the defense and
intelligence establishment into the diplomatic service and the presidency's
own records. What it has *not* done, five tranches in, is release anything
touching the deeper claims of the 2023 testimony.

## The Wheel of Heaven reading

The [Age of Aquarius](/timeline/age-of-aquarius/) chapter's ninth section
anticipates, for the present decades, an acceleration in how officially the
UAP question is handled — the corpus's dispatches read the five releases as
five datable points on that curve, and PURSUE itself as the curve's clearest
institutional expression to date.

The framework's interest is specific. On the canon's account, contact — when
and if it comes — will be **official, governmental, and conditional**: a
diplomatic event, for which the source material's standing request is the
construction of an [embassy](../embassy/). A disclosure infrastructure whose
contributing roster now includes the State Department and the Executive
Office of the President is therefore legible to the framework in a way a
sensor-video release alone would not be: the institutions through which any
official contact would run are, at minimum, now on the record as handling
the question.

The registration stops there. Under the corpus's reception posture, PURSUE's
unresolved cases confirm nothing for the framework, and its debunked cases
refute nothing in it — the framework's provenance rests on the canon's
reports, not on the archive. The posture names a single exception it does not
firewall: an **official, verified disclosure event**. PURSUE is not that
event. It is an archive opening on a schedule, and the corpus tracks it as
exactly that.

## Limits and criticism

**Unresolved is not non-human.** The archive selects for the cases the
government failed to identify — a category that grows with sensor coverage
and observation density. Nothing in the unresolved framing implies a
non-conventional explanation, and the Department itself characterizes no
released case as confirmed non-conventional.

**The deeper claims remain untouched.** Five tranches contain no material on
the craft-retrieval and reverse-engineering allegations of the 2023
testimony. Two readings are available — that such material does not exist to
be released (AARO's stated finding), or that it exists outside this program's
reach (the whistleblower position, resting significantly on the still-open
contractor question in the ODNI guidance). The corpus holds both open and
enlists neither.

**Much of the material is not new.** Substantial portions of the releases
were previously known through FOIA productions, Blue Book files, and the
historical literature; what PURSUE adds is official consolidation, indexing,
and provenance. Critics read the program as transparency theater — recycled
material under presidential branding; supporters answer that indexed,
citable, official publication is precisely what the FOIA era never produced.

**The record-keeping is loose at the edges.** The Department published no
file-count breakdown for its fifth release, press tallies and the portal's
own index disagree on dates, and announcement-versus-index attribution of
files differs across tranches. The corpus's dispatches attribute counts
rather than assert them.

**Durability is the real test.** A program four months old, carrying one
president's name, proves little by cadence alone. The framework's own
load-bearing question — argued across the dispatch series — is whether the
disclosure curve survives on a multi-year horizon, across administrations and
news cycles. That question is open.
