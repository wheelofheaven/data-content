The **Tabernacle** (Hebrew <span class="hebrew">מִשְׁכָּן</span>, *mishkan*, "dwelling") is the portable sanctuary whose construction the book of Exodus specifies in chapters 25–31 and narrates in chapters 35–40; the text also calls it the *ohel moʿed*, the "tent of meeting". {% claim(type="framework", id="woh-claim-0075") %}The Raëlian canon calls the tent a residence the Israelites built to the creators' own plans, for a team of [Elohim](../elohim/) staying on Earth for a time{% end %}.

## Name and terms

The Hebrew *mishkan* is a noun of place from the root *š-k-n*, "to settle, to dwell". The instruction that opens the section gives the purpose in the same root: "let them make me a sanctuary, that I may dwell among them" (Exodus 25:8), and the theme returns at the close of the priestly ordination laws: "I will dwell among the children of Israel, and will be their God" (Exodus 29:45). Three other names run alongside it. *Miqdash*, "sanctuary" or "holy place", stresses separation. *Ohel moʿed*, "tent of meeting" or "tent of appointment", stresses the encounter that takes place there. *Ohel ha-ʿedut*, "tent of the testimony", names it after the tablets kept inside.

The English word comes from the Latin *tabernaculum*, "tent, hut", which renders the Greek *skēnē* of the Septuagint. Later rabbinic Hebrew formed *Shekhinah*, the indwelling presence of God, from the same root as *mishkan*.

One more term carries weight in the text and in the canon's reading. *Tavnit*, "pattern" or "model", is what [Moses](../moses/) is shown on the mountain: "According to all that I show thee, the pattern of the tabernacle, and the pattern of all the furniture thereof, even so shall ye make it" (Exodus 25:9; compare 25:40).

## The Tabernacle in Exodus

The section opens with a voluntary offering. "Of every man whose heart maketh him willing ye shall take my offering" (Exodus 25:2): gold, silver and bronze, blue, purple and scarlet yarn, fine linen, goats' hair, rams' skins, acacia wood, oil, spices and precious stones (25:3–7). The instructions then proceed from the inside out. First come the furnishings of the innermost room: the [Ark](../ark-of-the-covenant/) and its cover with the two cherubim (25:10–22). Then come the table (25:23–30) and the lampstand, the [Menorah](../menorah/) (25:31–40). Chapter 26 gives the dwelling itself: ten curtains of twined linen worked with cherubim, a covering of goats' hair and skins, a frame of acacia boards, and a veil dividing the holy place from the most holy. Chapter 27 adds the bronze altar and the courtyard. Chapters 28–30 deal with the priestly vestments, the ordination of Aaron and his sons, the incense altar and the laver.

Exodus 31 names the craftsmen, Bezalel of the tribe of Judah and Oholiab of the tribe of Dan (31:2, 31:6). Chapters 35–40 then repeat the instructions almost word for word as a report of their execution. The dwelling is set up "in the first month in the second year, on the first day of the month" (40:17). The book closes with the cloud covering the tent of meeting and "the glory of Jehovah" filling the Tabernacle so that Moses cannot enter (40:34–35).

The structure is the centre of the rest of the Priestly legislation. Leviticus sets out the offerings brought to it and the rules for those who approach it. Numbers arranges the tribes around it: "over against the tent of meeting shall they encamp round about" (Numbers 2:2). The Levites dismantle, carry and erect it on the march (Numbers 3–4).

### The tent outside the camp

A second tradition stands beside this one. In Exodus 33, after the golden-calf episode, "Moses used to take the tent and to pitch it without the camp, afar off from the camp; and he called it, The tent of meeting" (33:7). When Moses enters, "the pillar of cloud descended, and stood at the door of the Tent" (33:9), and "Jehovah spake unto Moses face to face, as a man speaketh unto his friend" (33:11). Joshua, not a priest, keeps the tent (33:11). The same picture appears in Numbers 11–12, where the seventy elders are gathered "round about the Tent" and the cloud descends at its door, and in Deuteronomy 31:14–15. This tent stands outside the camp. Its function is oracular, it has no furnishings or sacrifices, and its attendant is not a priest. The Priestly Mishkan stands at the centre of the camp, served by Aaron's line.

## Composition and history

Modern critical scholarship assigns Exodus 25–31 and 35–40 to the Priestly source (P), one of the strands the documentary hypothesis distinguishes in the Pentateuch. The decisive formulation came from Julius Wellhausen, the German biblical scholar and orientalist, in his *Prolegomena to the History of Israel* (first published in 1878). Wellhausen argued that the Tabernacle has no history before the Priestly writer. On his reading, the older narrative sources know only a simple tent, and P took the centralized Temple of Jerusalem, made it portable and projected it back into the wilderness, so that the cult of the Second Temple period could claim Mosaic origin. This retrojection reading remains the baseline of the historical-critical account.

Richard Elliott Friedman, the American biblical scholar whose *Who Wrote the Bible?* (1987) presented the documentary hypothesis to a general readership, sets out the Priestly attribution and the source-critical distinction between the oracle tent of Exodus 33 and the Priestly Mishkan. Not every critic follows Wellhausen on the structure itself. Some have argued that the Priestly description preserves an actual tent shrine older than the Temple, which would give the specifications a historical referent that Wellhausen's account denies. The question remains open in the scholarship.

Archaeology has not settled the wilderness period in favour of either. Israel Finkelstein, the Tel Aviv University archaeologist, and Neil Asher Silberman report in *The Bible Unearthed* (2001) that the sites associated with the wandering have yielded no remains of a large population in the period the narrative implies. They read the Exodus and wilderness stories as compositions of the monarchic period. The framework makes no claim about the historicity or the dating of the wilderness period.

## In Jewish and Christian tradition

In Jewish thought the Mishkan is the model of divine presence in the midst of the people. Benjamin D. Sommer, the Jewish Theological Seminary scholar of the Hebrew Bible, reads the Priestly chapters in *The Bodies of God and the World of Ancient Israel* (2009) as a theology of the *kavod*, the divine glory, dwelling in a particular place. He sets this against the Deuteronomic tradition, which locates only God's name in the sanctuary. Rabbinic literature carried the presence forward under the name *Shekhinah*, and taught that wherever Israel was exiled the Shekhinah went with them (Babylonian Talmud, Megillah 29a). In the Kabbalah the Shekhinah became the tenth and last of the divine emanations, as Gershom Scholem, the Hebrew University historian who founded the academic study of Jewish mysticism, describes in *Major Trends in Jewish Mysticism* (1941). The Tabernacle work also shaped Jewish law. Because the Torah sets the Sabbath command beside it (Exodus 31:12–17; 35:1–3), the rabbis derived the thirty-nine categories of work forbidden on the Sabbath, listed in Mishnah Shabbat 7:2, from the labours that built the Tabernacle.

Christian reading turned the Tabernacle into typology. The Epistle to the Hebrews calls the priests' service "a copy and shadow of the heavenly things", citing the instruction to make all things "according to the pattern that was showed thee in the mount" (Hebrews 8:5). Christ enters "through the greater and more perfect tabernacle, not made with hands" (9:11). The Gospel of John says the Word "dwelt among us" (John 1:14), using the Greek verb *skēnoō*, from *skēnē*, "tent". Both traditions are carried here in their own terms. The canon's reading does not replace them.

## In the Wheel of Heaven reading

{% claim(type="framework", id="woh-claim-0053") %}The framework reads the [Hebrew Bible](../hebrew-bible/) as a compressed, transmitted and theologically reworked record of the creators' engagement with humanity{% end %}. That posture licenses no specific reading on its own. The reading of the Tabernacle stands on its own record, which follows the canon's text in *The Book Which Tells the Truth* (Raël, 1974), chapter 3.

### A residence built to plan

The canon's account opens with the offering. {% claim(type="framework", id="woh-claim-0075") %}A team of creators "was going to reside for a time on Earth and wanted fresh food", and asked the people of Israel to supply it regularly, together with riches to take back to its planet{% end %} (TBWTT-3:34). The canon calls this "a bit of colonization, if you like" and quotes the list of Exodus 25:2–7 (TBWTT-3:35). It then reads the structure:

> Ils avaient également décidé de s'installer plus confortablement et demandèrent aux hommes de leur confectionner une résidence suivant leurs plans. C'est ce qui est dicté au chapitre XXVI de l'Exode. Dans cette résidence ils devaient rencontrer les représentants des hommes : c'est la tente du rendez-vous où les hommes apportaient nourriture et présents en gage de soumission.
>
> *They had also decided to settle more comfortably and asked men to fashion a residence for them according to their plans. This is what is dictated in chapter XXVI of Exodus. In this residence they were to meet the representatives of men: it is the tent of meeting, where men brought food and presents as a pledge of submission.* (TBWTT-3:36)

{% claim(type="framework", id="woh-claim-0075") %}The canon reads the Tabernacle as a residence built to the creators' plans, the meeting place between the creators and the people's representatives, and the place where food and goods were delivered{% end %}. The "pattern" shown on the mountain becomes, on this reading, a set of plans. The offerings become provisioning. The canon extends the reading to the Levitical rules on who may approach: it says that the beginning of Leviticus "explains how the foods offered to the creators must be brought for their provisioning". It quotes Leviticus 21:17, where a descendant of Aaron with a blemish may not approach to offer "the food of his God", as a rule governing who came before the creators (TBWTT-3:43–45).

### Face to face, and the suit

The canon quotes Exodus 33:9 and 33:11, the cloud at the door of the tent and Yahweh speaking with Moses "face to face, as a man speaketh unto his friend", and glosses it: "Just as today I can speak to you and you can speak to me, man to man" (TBWTT-3:38–40). It then sets beside it the refusal of Exodus 33:20, "Thou canst not see my face: for there shall no man see me, and live". {% claim(type="framework", id="woh-claim-0075") %}The canon resolves the two verses through a difference of atmosphere between the planets: a human could meet [Yahweh](../yahweh/) at the tent only while the creator was protected by a suit{% end %} (TBWTT-3:41–42).

### Two modes of contact

The canon distinguishes two ways the creators kept in touch with Israel's leaders. Telepathy was possible "only when the Elohim were in proximity to Earth". When they were on their distant planet or elsewhere, they used "a transmitter-receiver that was carried in the 'ark of God'" (TBWTT-3:95–96). The tent of meeting belongs to the first mode: presence and direct exchange while the creators were near. {% claim(type="framework", id="woh-claim-0040") %}The Ark, kept within the Tabernacle, is read as a communication device for the times the creators were far away{% end %}. That reading is the [Ark of the Covenant](../ark-of-the-covenant/) entry's, and it extends to that one artifact only. The altars, the table and the laver have no framework reading.

The lampstand of Exodus 25:31–40 has its own reading. {% claim(type="framework", id="woh-claim-0069") %}The canon calls the seven-branched lampstand the lost memory of a console at the creators' headquarters, whose seven indicator lights kept contact with their other bases and with the craft in orbit{% end %} (TBWTT-3:294). The [Menorah](../menorah/) entry treats that reading and its alternatives.

### What the reading does not claim

The canon draws on both tent traditions at once. It cites Exodus 26 for the residence, which belongs to the Priestly Mishkan, and Exodus 33 for the face-to-face meetings, which belongs to the tent outside the camp. The framework registers that the canon merges the two. It does not settle the source-critical question of how they relate, and it offers no reconstruction of the structure, its materials or its dimensions beyond what the canon states.

The canon reads the "glory" as the creators' craft at Sinai. It quotes Exodus 24:17, the glory "like devouring fire on the top of the mount", and says that the craft "takes on a coloring similar to that of fire" when it departs (TBWTT-3:32–33). It does not make that reading of the cloud and glory that fill the Tabernacle at Exodus 40:34–38, and the framework does not extend it there.

The canon heads its account of Solomon's Temple "the first residence for the reception of the Elohim" (TBWTT-3:102), which sits uneasily beside its calling the tent a residence. The framework records no line of continuity running from the Tabernacle through the Temples, and the Tabernacle reading carries no claim about the [Embassy](../embassy/).

## Alternatives and critical views

**Priestly retrojection.** On the Wellhausen line, the Tabernacle of Exodus 25–31 and 35–40 is the priestly writer's Temple projected into the wilderness. No desert structure stands behind the specifications, and a reading that treats them as a builder's plans takes a theological composition for a technical document.

**Two tents.** Source critics separate the oracle tent pitched outside the camp (Exodus 33:7–11; Numbers 11–12) from the Priestly Mishkan at the camp's centre. On this view, the canon's reading depends on a harmonization the texts themselves resist, since it takes its plans from one tradition and its face-to-face meetings from the other.

**Cultic idiom.** "The bread of his God" (Leviticus 21:17, in the ASV wording) and "my food for my offerings made by fire, of a sweet savor unto me" (Numbers 28:2) belong to the ordinary vocabulary of ancient Near Eastern temple cult, in which offerings are spoken of as the deity's food. Read this way, the language expresses a theology of divine dwelling and says nothing about the provisioning of resident beings. This objection is aimed directly at the provisioning clause.

**Speculation lineage.** Susan J. Palmer, the Canadian sociologist of new religious movements, studies the Raëlian movement in *Aliens Adored* (2004), including its reading of the Bible as an account of extraterrestrial contact. Critics place the residence-and-provisioning reading within the 1970s ancient-astronaut register: on that view the reading reflects the interests of its period, and the texts do not yield it. The same objection is raised against the Ark reading.
