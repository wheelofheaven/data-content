{% claim(type="direct", id="woh-claim-0077") %}**Buddha** is the figure the Raëlian canon names among the messengers of the [Elohim](../elohim/), seated at [Jesus](../jesus/)'s left at [Yahweh](../yahweh/)'s table on the planet of the Eternals.{% end %} The corpus identifies him with **Siddhārtha Gautama** (Pali *Siddhattha Gotama*), called Śākyamuni, the teacher of the central Ganges plain whose community became Buddhism, and whose life most scholars place in the fifth century BCE or a little earlier. That identification is the corpus's, made by editorial decision of the project's founder; the canon refers only to "the one whom, on Earth, one remembers under the name of Buddha."

## The name and the title

*Buddha* is a title, not a personal name. It is the past participle of the Sanskrit and Pali verbal root *budh*, "to wake up, to come to understand," and means "one who has awakened." The Buddhist traditions use it of anyone who has reached full awakening by his own effort and then taught the way to others. They name buddhas before Gautama, and the early texts already speak of a succession of them across vast ages.{{ cite(id="buswell-lopez-princeton-buddhism", text="[7]", title="Buswell & Lopez, The Princeton Dictionary of Buddhism, s.v. buddha") }} The Mahāyāna traditions go further and describe buddhas presiding over other world systems in the present, as Paul Williams, a scholar of Indian and Tibetan Buddhism at the University of Bristol, sets out in his standard introduction to Mahāyāna doctrine.{{ cite(id="williams-mahayana", text="[8]", title="Williams, Mahāyāna Buddhism: The Doctrinal Foundations") }}

The historical teacher's personal name was Siddhārtha ("he who has achieved his aim"), his clan name Gautama. *Śākyamuni*, "the sage of the Śākyas," places him in the Śākya people of the Himalayan foothills. Within the texts he most often calls himself *Tathāgata*, "the one thus gone," and his followers address him as *Bhagavat*, "the Blessed One." The French of the canon writes *Bouddha*.

## Life as the tradition remembers it

The traditional biography is assembled from the Pali discourses, the monastic codes and later narrative lives. Rupert Gethin, scholar of Buddhist studies at the University of Bristol, sets the account out as the tradition preserves it, and it is given here in that form; how much of it is historical cannot be settled from the texts alone.{{ cite(id="gethin-foundations-of-buddhism", text="[6]", title="Gethin, The Foundations of Buddhism, ch. 1") }} On this account Siddhārtha was born at Lumbinī to Śuddhodana, a chief of the Śākyas at Kapilavastu, and to Māyā. Raised in comfort, he left home as a young man to seek release from old age, sickness and death. He studied meditation under two teachers and practiced severe austerities, then abandoned both as insufficient.

Seated beneath a fig tree at what is now Bodh Gayā, in Bihar, he reached the awakening (*bodhi*) from which the title derives. He gave his first teaching to five former companions in the deer park at Sarnath, near Vārāṇasī, and spent some four and a half decades teaching across the kingdoms of Magadha and Kosala and founding an order of monks and, later, nuns. He died at Kuśinagara at about eighty. The *Mahāparinibbāna Sutta*, the sixteenth discourse of the Pali *Dīgha Nikāya*, narrates his last journey, his final instructions and his death, which the tradition calls the *parinirvāṇa*, the complete extinction.{{ cite(id="4", text="[4]", title="Walshe (trans.), The Long Discourses of the Buddha, DN 16") }}

The teaching itself is summarized in the four noble truths: suffering, its origin in craving, its cessation, and the eightfold path to that cessation. It is set within the doctrines of no-self (*anattā*), dependent origination and karma, and directed at *nirvāṇa* (Pali *nibbāna*), the extinguishing of the craving that drives rebirth in [saṃsāra](../samsara/).{{ cite(id="gethin-foundations-of-buddhism", text="[6]", title="Gethin, The Foundations of Buddhism, chs. 3–4") }} The Buddha wrote nothing. His teaching was transmitted orally, and the Pali tradition records that it was first committed to writing in Sri Lanka in the first century BCE.{{ cite(id="gethin-foundations-of-buddhism", text="[6]", title="Gethin, The Foundations of Buddhism, ch. 2") }}

## Dating: long and short chronologies

No date in the Buddha's life is fixed by a contemporary record. Two families of chronology derive from two textual traditions, and both remain scholarly positions.

The **long chronology** rests on the Sri Lankan chronicles, which count 218 years from the Buddha's death to the consecration of the emperor Aśoka. The traditional Theravāda reckoning places the death in 544/543 BCE. Western scholarship long preferred a corrected form of the same count, which places it around 486–483 BCE and the birth some eighty years earlier, around 566–563 BCE.

The **short chronology** rests on northern Buddhist sources preserved in Sanskrit, Chinese and Tibetan, which put roughly a century between the death and Aśoka. It places the death around 400 BCE or later. At the Göttingen symposium convened by the Indologist Heinz Bechert in 1988, whose papers appeared in three volumes between 1991 and 1997, most contributors moved the death well after the corrected long date, many into a window around 400 BCE.{{ cite(id="9", text="[9]", title="Bechert (ed.), The Dating of the Historical Buddha") }}

Archaeology bears on the question without settling it. Aśoka's pillar at Rummindei, in the Nepalese Tarai, records that the emperor came in person to worship at the site in the twentieth year after his consecration, around the middle of the third century BCE, because the Buddha, the Śākya sage, was born there.{{ cite(id="5", text="[5]", title="Hultzsch, Inscriptions of Asoka, Rummindei pillar") }} Excavations beneath the Māyā Devī temple at Lumbinī, led by the archaeologist Robin Coningham of Durham University, reported a timber structure that the team dated to the sixth century BCE and interpreted as an early Buddhist shrine. The excavators argued that the finding favors an earlier date for the Buddha. Others note that a dated structure under a later shrine does not by itself date the person it later commemorated.{{ cite(id="10", text="[10]", title="Coningham et al., The earliest Buddhist shrine, Antiquity, 2013") }}

## In the Wheel of Heaven reading

### The table and the line of the prophets

The canon names Buddha twice in the dictated second Message, *[Extraterrestrials Took Me to Their Planet](/library/extraterrestrials-took-me-to-their-planet/)* (1975). In the first passage, Raël is brought to a meal on the planet of the Eternals, seated facing Jesus, "whom we were able to recreate from a cell we had preserved before his crucifixion." Yahweh then presents the others at the table:

{% library(book="extraterrestrials-took-me-to-their-planet", chapter=2, verse=64) %}
At his right is found Moses, at his left Elijah, at the left of Jesus is seated the one whom, on Earth, one remembers under the name of Buddha. A little further you can see Mohammed in whose writings I am called Allah for he did not dare to name me out of respect. The forty men and women present at this meal are all beings representative of the religions created following our contacts on the Earth.
{% end %}

The canon places Buddha at Jesus's left, with [Moses](../moses/) at Jesus's right, [Elijah](../elijah/) at his left and [Muhammad](../muhammad/) "a little further." The French reads *la quarantaine*, "the forty or so." The text describes how Jesus came to be present and says nothing of how Buddha or the others came to be there. It gives no list of the forty and no order among them. The corpus seats no one at the table beyond those the text places there, and assigns no figure a number in any sequence.

The second passage comes in the Message's final pages. Yahweh says of Raël that "he has not written what Moses, Ezekiel, Elijah, Jesus, Mohammed, Buddha and all the others wrote … but he is the last of the line of the prophets."{{ cite(id="extraterrestrials-took-me-to-their-planet", text="[1]", title="Extraterrestrials Took Me to Their Planet, ETTMTTP-3:257") }} Buddha stands in that line as one of Raël's predecessors.

{% claim(type="framework", id="woh-claim-0038") %}Per the canon, the prophets of the world's traditions were operational interlocutors between the Elohim and particular peoples and eras: contacted, briefed and tasked, each delivering the message in the register his era and culture could carry.{% end %} Buddha belongs to that sequence because the canon names him in it. The canon gives no account of his contact or his briefing, and the corpus supplies none. On this reading, the differences between Buddhism and the Abrahamic traditions are the era-adaptation the role definition describes (see [Prophet](../prophet/)); each tradition's own account of its founder stands in its own terms.

### Raël's commentary voice and the expected return

In *[Let's Welcome the Extraterrestrials](/library/lets-welcome-the-extraterrestrials/)* (1979), Raël names Buddha again, this time in his own commentary voice, which the corpus treats as canon but always marks as distinct from the dictated Messages. In the chapter "An Atheist Religion" he writes that Raëlians "recognize Jesus as a messenger sent by our creators, just as they do Moses, Buddha, Mohammed, Joseph Smith, and all the other great prophets," and "await their return in the company of the Elohim" (LWTE-3:27).{{ cite(id="lets-welcome-the-extraterrestrials", text="[2]", title="Let's Welcome the Extraterrestrials, LWTE-3:27") }} Twice more he names Buddha among those who will come with the Elohim to the [Embassy](../embassy/): "the ancient messengers: Moses, Jesus, Buddha, and Mohammed" (LWTE-1:87) and "the great prophets, Moses, Jesus, Buddha, Mohammed and others" (LWTE-3:82). A reader's question in the same book (LWTE-1:75) presupposes the prophets living on the planet of the Eternals; that is the questioner's premise, which Raël's answer does not dispute.

### The Buddhist cycle at the scale of humanity

The longest engagement with Buddhist teaching in the canon is a single paragraph of the second Message, which opens "This is what Buddhism explains." Yahweh summarizes the Buddhist account of death: the dying "soul" must be vigilant enough to escape the "demons," or it is reborn and falls back into the cycle. {% claim(type="framework", id="woh-claim-0048") %}The canon calls this "a very good description which applies not to the individual but to humanity entire": the demons are "aggressiveness against one's fellows or against the nature in which one lives," falling back into the cycle is a civilization's return to the primitive stage and "a new slow progression," and "the state of beatitude through awakening is the golden age of civilizations where science is at the service of men."{% end %} (ETTMTTP-2:24; see [Golden Age](../golden-age/).){{ cite(id="extraterrestrials-took-me-to-their-planet", text="[1]", title="Extraterrestrials Took Me to Their Planet, ETTMTTP-2:24") }}

The re-reading concerns the cycle and its escape. The canon gives no account of karma as moral accounting or of the metaphysics of nirvāṇa, and the corpus does not supply one. In the Buddhist traditions the cycle remains the rebirth of individual beings, driven by craving and ended in nirvāṇa, and the canon's humanity-scale reading does not replace that account. Separately, and on its own passages, the canon promises individual "scientific reincarnation" on the planet of the Eternals (ETTMTTP-3:208, 3:214). It does not attach that promise to the Buddhism paragraph. Set beside Buddhist rebirth, the promise meets a standing objection: the doctrine of no-self denies the enduring self that the recreation of the same being presupposes.

{% claim(type="framework", id="woh-claim-0043") %}Near its close the same paragraph turns to the emblem: "It is for this that in our emblem figures the svastika or gammadion cross which one finds in numerous ancient writings and which signifies the cycle."{% end %} The svastika is an auspicious sign in Buddhist, Hindu and Jain iconography. That wide distribution has independent explanations and is not evidence for the canon's reading. The sign's appropriation by National Socialism is the modern context any use of it now carries, treated in the [Swastika](../swastika/) entry.

### "The new Buddha must be born in the West"

Near its end the second Message addresses each tradition in turn. The Christian has read "a hundred times that Jesus would return"; the child of Israel "still waits for your messiah"; and to the Buddhist Yahweh says: "your writings indicate that the new Buddha must be born in the West; recognize the foreseen signs!" The Muslim is told to "welcome the last of the prophets" (ETTMTTP-3:226–229). {% claim(type="inferred", id="woh-claim-0078") %}Read in that sequence, and with ETTMTTP-3:257, where Raël is "the last of the line of the prophets," the corpus takes the Buddhist clause as pointing at [Raël](../rael/).{% end %} Only the Muslim clause names "the last of the prophets"; the Buddhist clause points by parallel, which is why the reading is labelled *inferred*. The corpus makes no claim about what Buddhist scripture predicts, and the canon's "new Buddha" is identified with no figure of Buddhist tradition.

### The traditions that testify

In the first Message, *[The Book Which Tells the Truth](/library/the-book-which-tells-the-truth/)* (1974), the Elohim list "the Buddhist religion, the Islamic, the Mormons" among the religions "that testify in a more or less obscure way to our work" (TBWTT-5:54).{{ cite(id="the-book-which-tells-the-truth", text="[3]", title="The Book Which Tells the Truth, TBWTT-5:54") }} {% claim(type="direct", id="woh-claim-0027") %}The canon presents itself as the demystified common source of the existing religions, naming Buddhism among the traditions that testify to the same creators.{% end %} This account of Buddhism is the canon's, and it carries no claim that Buddhists accept it.

## Alternative and critical readings

**The referent of "Buddha."** Because *buddha* is a title borne by many figures in Buddhist tradition, the canon's phrase, "the one whom, on Earth, one remembers under the name of Buddha," does not by itself fix who is meant. The identification with Siddhārtha Gautama is the corpus's reading, adopted by the founder's editorial decision, and is no statement of the canon.

**The lists follow the audience.** A deflationary reading holds that the canon's named lists track the religious map of a 1970s French audience and record no independent fact about the figures, as the same objection argues for the prophetic sequence as a whole. A related objection holds that prophet sequences are developments internal to the traditions, Islamic prophetology above all, and that the canon generalizes a pattern they built for their own reasons. Susan J. Palmer, the sociologist of religion whose fieldwork monograph *Aliens Adored* (2004) is the principal academic account of the movement, provides the context in which such readings are argued.{{ cite(id="aliens-adored", text="[11]", title="Palmer, Aliens Adored: Raël's UFO Religion") }}

**No western birth.** The strongest objection to the "new Buddha" reading concerns the canon's characterization of Buddhist writings. The Pāli account of the next buddha, the Discourse on the Wheel-Turning Monarch (Dīgha Nikāya 26), places Metteyya's arising in Ketumatī, the renamed Varanasi, in an age when humans live 80,000 years, and nothing in it places him in the West. Read against that text, the canon describes the Buddhist writings in a way the Pāli account does not bear out. Later Buddhist literature on the future buddha has not been checked.

**Exhortation, not identification.** The address of ETTMTTP-3:226–229 can be read as exhortation, urging readers of each tradition to take the Message seriously, with no doctrine of identification behind it. On that reading, drawing a designation of Raël out of the Buddhist clause over-reads rhetoric.

**The tradition's own voice.** For Buddhists the Buddha's awakening and the doctrine of no-self are the center of the teaching. A reading that seats him at a table of embodied creators, or that turns awakening into the golden age of a scientific civilization, describes something the tradition does not recognize in its own terms. The corpus records the re-reading as the canon's and sets the tradition's account beside it.
