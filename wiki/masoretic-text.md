The **Masoretic Text** (MT) is the authoritative Hebrew text of the
{% wiki(slug="hebrew-bible") %}Hebrew Bible{% end %} (Tanakh): the ancient
consonantal text together with the vowel-pointing, cantillation, and marginal
notes fixed by the **Masoretes** — the Jewish scribe-scholars of the early
medieval period, and above all the **Ben Asher** family of Tiberias, working
between roughly the seventh and tenth centuries CE. It is the text printed in
nearly every modern Hebrew Bible and the base from which most contemporary
translations of the Old Testament are made. Its two great manuscripts, the
Aleppo Codex and the Leningrad Codex, anchor the scholarly editions used across
the field.

The name comes from the Hebrew *masorah* (מָסוֹרָה), "tradition" — the body of
transmitted scribal detail the Masoretes recorded in order to fix the text and
guard it against the drift that copying introduces. A Masorete was, in the plain
sense of the word, a keeper of that tradition.

## What the Masoretes added

Biblical Hebrew was written in consonants alone. The letters of the Tanakh —
the *consonantal text* — are old, and by the late Second Temple period they were
substantially fixed; what they did not record was how the words were to be
pronounced, or how the clauses were to be divided. Vocalization lived in an oral
reading tradition passed from teacher to student. The Masoretes' achievement was
to write that oral tradition down and attach it to the text without altering a
single consonant.

Three layers make up their work. The **vowel points** (*niqqud*, נִקּוּד) are a
system of dots and strokes placed above, below, and inside the consonants to fix
the pronunciation. The **cantillation accents** (*te'amim*, טְעָמִים) mark the
liturgical chant and, in doing so, also encode the syntax — where a verse
pauses, which words bind together, where the sense breaks. The **Masorah**
proper is the apparatus of marginal and terminal notes: counts of letters and
words, flags on unusual or unique forms, and the *ketiv/qere* readings that
record where the written text (*ketiv*) and the text as read aloud (*qere*)
diverge. Together these turned an unpointed scroll into a text that carried its
own reading instructions.

Several regional schools developed competing vocalization systems — Babylonian
and Palestinian among them — but the **Tiberian** system, perfected in the
Galilean town of Tiberias, prevailed. Its most authoritative form is associated
with **Aaron ben Moshe ben Asher** (10th century), the last of a dynasty of
Tiberian Masoretes, and it is the ben Asher tradition that stands behind the
manuscripts the field now treats as standard.

## The great codices

Two manuscripts carry the tradition. The **Aleppo Codex** (*Keter Aram Tzova*,
c. 930 CE) was vocalized and annotated by Aaron ben Asher himself and was
regarded for centuries as the most accurate copy of the Tanakh in existence;
Maimonides took it as his model. It was kept for generations by the Jewish
community of Aleppo, and in the 1947 anti-Jewish riots there a substantial
portion — including most of the Torah — was lost. What survives is still the
single most authoritative witness to the Tiberian text and the base for the
Hebrew University Bible Project.

The **Leningrad Codex** (Codex Leningradensis, firkovich B19a, dated 1008 CE) is
the oldest *complete* manuscript of the Hebrew Bible in the Tiberian tradition.
Because it is intact where the Aleppo Codex is damaged, it became the diplomatic
base of the standard critical editions: the *Biblia Hebraica Stuttgartensia*
(BHS) and its successor *Biblia Hebraica Quinta* (BHQ), the working Hebrew Bibles
of contemporary scholarship. When a modern reader consults "the Hebrew text," it
is, in practice, almost always the Leningrad Codex that lies underneath.

## The standard base text

The MT is the received text of the Hebrew Bible — the version transmitted within
Judaism and inherited, through translation, by the Christian traditions. Its
transmission was remarkably stable: the Masoretes' obsessive counting and
cross-checking, and the reverence in which the consonantal text was held, kept
copying error to a minimum across the medieval period. The **Dead Sea Scrolls**
discovered at Qumran and nearby sites pushed the manuscript evidence back roughly
a thousand years and broadly confirmed that stability; the Great Isaiah Scroll
(1QIsaᵃ, c. 125 BCE) matches the Masoretic Isaiah closely across more than a
millennium of copying. Emanuel Tov, the Israeli textual critic whose *Textual
Criticism of the Hebrew Bible* is the standard synthesis of the field, treats the
MT as the single most important witness to the text while insisting that it is a
witness, not the autograph — one branch of a transmission that also runs through
the Greek and the Qumran manuscripts.

## A medieval vocalization over an ancient consonantal frame

Here lies the caveat that matters most for close reading. The two components of
the MT are not the same age. The consonants are ancient — attested a thousand
years before the Masoretes in the Qumran scrolls, and standing behind the Hebrew
*Vorlage* the **Septuagint** translators rendered into Greek in the third and
second centuries BCE. The vowel-points and accents, by contrast, are *medieval*:
the earliest complete pointed manuscripts date only to the tenth and eleventh
centuries CE. The MT's vocalization is therefore later than the consonantal
witnesses it sits on top of — a fixing, in the early Middle Ages, of one reading
tradition among the several the consonants could in principle support.

For most of the text this makes no practical difference; the reading tradition
the Masoretes recorded was old and well-controlled. But it means that a vowel or
an accent is, formally, the Masoretes' interpretation of how an unpointed word
should be read, and where the consonants are ambiguous the pointing represents a
choice. Text critics accordingly weigh the MT against the older witnesses rather
than treating it as automatically decisive, and where the MT and those witnesses
diverge the difference can carry weight.

The best-known case is {% libref(book="deuteronomy", chapter=32, verse=8) %}Deuteronomy
32:8{% end %}. The MT reads that the Most High divided the nations "according to
the number of the **sons of Israel**" (*bene Yisrael*); a Qumran fragment
(4QDeut(j)) and the Septuagint instead read "sons of God" (*bene Elohim*) or
"angels of God." Most textual critics judge the shorter, stranger reading the
older one and the Masoretic "sons of Israel" a later adjustment — a case, on the
mainstream analysis, in which the consonantal witnesses behind the Greek and the
Scroll preserve something the received Hebrew text smoothed away. Genesis and the
rest of the {% wiki(slug="genesis") %}primeval history{% end %} show far less of
this kind of divergence, but the Deuteronomy example is the standing reminder
that the MT is one witness with a datable editorial history, not a fixed point
outside of it.

## In the Wheel of Heaven framework

The framework's engagement with the MT is, first, methodological, and it stays
carefully inside what the text-critical facts allow. Mauro Biglino's strict-literal
reading — treated in the {% wiki(slug="biglino-method") %}Biglino Method{% end %}
entry, and in the biographical entry on {% wiki(slug="mauro-biglino") %}Mauro
Biglino{% end %} — works from the Masoretic *consonants* while treating the
vowel-pointing as a later interpretive layer that can be set aside where it
carries theological freight the consonants do not settle. Biglino himself came to
this from the translation desk: he spent years rendering the Masoretic Text into
Italian for a Catholic publisher, and his habit of quoting the Hebrew
consonants-only follows directly from the observation above, that the pointing is
the medieval reading tradition rather than the ancient text. This is a real
feature of the MT, not a framework claim; what is a framework claim is the
conclusion Biglino draws from reading the recovered consonants concretely, and
that conclusion is registered as his, and hedged, throughout the corpus.

Second, the divergences between the MT and the older witnesses are exactly where
the corpus watches most closely. The Deuteronomy 32:8 case above is read, on the
framework's account, as touching the {% wiki(slug="plurality-of-gods") %}plurality
of gods{% end %} question: the older "sons of God" reading fits a divine-council
picture that the Masoretic "sons of Israel" reads out of the verse. That the
older reading is likely original is a mainstream text-critical judgement; that it
preserves the memory of an actual plural company of creators is the framework's
interpretation, and it belongs to the corpus's reading rather than to the
manuscripts themselves. The distinction is the point. The manuscripts establish
that the MT and the older witnesses differ at this verse, and which reading is
probably earlier; the Wheel of Heaven reading of *why the difference matters* is
a separate, hedged step, argued at length in the Explainer
[*The Translator's Wager*](/articles/the-translators-wager/).

The corpus therefore treats the Masoretic Text as its Hebrew base — the received,
carefully transmitted consonantal text — while holding two disciplines alongside
it: read the consonants directly rather than through the medieval pointing where
the pointing is doing theological work, and consult the **Dead Sea Scrolls** and
the **Septuagint** where they preserve a reading the received Hebrew may have
adjusted. Neither move is peculiar to the framework; both are ordinary text
criticism. What the framework adds is the interpretive weight it places on the
recovered readings — a weight the mainstream witnesses do not carry on their own,
and which the corpus is careful to mark as its own.
