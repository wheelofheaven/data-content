The **Book of Enoch** — cited by scholars as **1 Enoch** to distinguish it from
the later 2 and 3 Enoch — is a composite Second Temple Jewish apocalyptic work
attributed to the antediluvian patriarch **Enoch**, the seventh figure of the
Genesis 5 genealogy, of whom the text says "he was not, for Elohim took him."
It is less a single book than a small library: five once-independent booklets
bound under one patriarch's name, composed in Aramaic and Hebrew between roughly
the third century BCE and the first century CE, and surviving complete only in
**Ge'ez** (classical Ethiopic), the language in which it remained canonical
scripture. For the Wheel of Heaven corpus its opening booklet, the **Book of the
Watchers** (chapters 1–36), is the most detailed ancient account of the events
that {% libref(book="genesis-woh", chapter=6, verse=1) %}Genesis 6:1–4{% end %}
compresses into four verses: the descent of the {% wiki(slug="watchers") %}Watchers{% end %},
their unions with human women, their transmission of forbidden arts, and the
giant {% wiki(slug="nephilim") %}Nephilim{% end %} whose violence precipitates
the {% wiki(slug="great-flood") %}Flood{% end %}.

This entry explains what 1 Enoch is and how the corpus reads it. The translated
text itself lives separately, at {% libref(book="book-of-enoch") %}the project's
Book of Enoch{% end %} — R. H. Charles's 1917 rendering of the Ethiopic, from
which the quotations below are drawn.

## Composition and survival

The transmission history follows the languages. The work was composed in
Aramaic (with the Book of Dreams and parts of the Epistle in Hebrew), translated
into Greek, and from Greek into Ge'ez. The complete text survives only in the
Ethiopic version, where 1 Enoch (*Metsehafe Henok*) has been continuously
copied and read as scripture in the Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo church — which
is the reason the book survives at all. The Greek is preserved in substantial
excerpts: the fifth-century Codex Panopolitanus recovered at Akhmim in 1886–87,
and the quotations of the Byzantine chronographer George Syncellus. The oldest
witnesses are the **Aramaic fragments** recovered from Qumran Cave 4 and
published by J. T. Milik in 1976, which proved that the Enochic booklets
circulated in Aramaic in the Second Temple period and pushed the composition of
the earliest strata back into the third century BCE — making the Book of the
Watchers older, in its final form, than the biblical book of Daniel.

The Western tradition lost the book entirely for more than a millennium, keeping
only the memory of a text that Jude had quoted. It re-entered European
scholarship when James Bruce brought Ethiopic manuscripts to Britain in 1773;
Richard Laurence published the first English translation in 1821, and Charles's
editions (1893, 1912, 1917) established the modern text. The Qumran discoveries
then supplied the Aramaic ground the Ethiopic transmission had long stood on
alone.

## The five books

The received 1 Enoch gathers five distinct compositions of different dates and
concerns, each with its own history:

- **The Book of the Watchers** (chapters 1–36) — the oldest and, for the corpus,
  the most consequential booklet: the descent of the Watchers, the forbidden
  arts, the giants, and the Flood. Treated in full below.
- **The Book of Parables**, or Similitudes (37–71) — three "parables" centred on
  a heavenly "Son of Man" and "Chosen One" figure. Alone among the five, it left
  no fragment at Qumran, and its date and provenance are the most contested in
  the corpus; it closes by identifying the exalted figure with Enoch himself.
- **The Astronomical Book**, or Book of the Luminaries (72–82) — a detailed
  treatise on the movements of sun, moon, and stars advocating a 364-day solar
  calendar. It is the earliest Enochic material attested at Qumran and shows the
  tradition's roots in ancient Near Eastern celestial science.
- **The Book of Dreams** (83–90) — two visions, the second of which, the "Animal
  Apocalypse," allegorizes the whole of Israelite history as a succession of
  animals down to the Maccabean period.
- **The Epistle of Enoch** (91–108) — a testamentary section of exhortation and
  woe, containing the "Apocalypse of Weeks," which periodizes history into ten
  weeks running to a final judgment.

The booklets share a persona and a cosmology but not a single argument; the
corpus's reading turns almost entirely on the first of them.

## The Book of the Watchers

Chapters 6–11 carry the core narrative. Two hundred Watchers, "the children of
the heaven," see the daughters of men and resolve to take them as wives; their
leader **Shemihazah**, fearing he alone will bear the guilt, binds the company
by mutual oath on the summit of **Mount Hermon** — the text deriving the
mountain's name from the oath (*ḥerem*) sworn there.

{% library(book="book-of-enoch", chapter=6, verse=1) %}
And it came to pass when the children of men had multiplied that in those days
were born unto them beautiful and comely daughters. And the angels, the children
of the heaven, saw and lusted after them, and said to one another: 'Come, let us
choose us wives from among the children of men and beget us children.'
{% end %}

Two things then follow, and the text litigates them separately. First, the
Watchers **teach**: under **Asael** (the Ethiopic Azâzêl) they transmit a
catalogue of arts — metallurgy and the making of swords, knives, and armour;
the working of ornaments, the use of antimony and cosmetics; dyes and precious
stones; enchantments and root-cutting; and the reading of the sun, moon, stars,
and clouds. Metallurgy, weaponry, pharmacology, cosmetic chemistry, sorcery, and
astrology arrive as a single package, and the text holds the teachers liable for
what their students do with the teaching: "the whole earth has been corrupted
through the works that were taught by Azâzêl." Second, the unions produce
**giants** — the Book of the Watchers' expansion of the Nephilim — who consume
the produce of human labour, then living things, then one another, until "the
earth laid accusation against the lawless ones."

The four archangels Michael, Uriel, Raphael, and Gabriel carry the earth's cry
upward, and the response is a sequence of commissions: Uriel is sent to warn
{% wiki(slug="noah") %}Noah{% end %} of the coming deluge; Raphael binds Azazel
in a desert pit; Gabriel sets the giants against one another; and Michael binds
Shemihazah's company "for seventy generations" until the final judgment. The
Book of the Watchers thereby supplies what Genesis leaves unstated — a causal
chain in which the Flood is the direct response to the descent, the teaching,
and the hybrids, rather than to unspecified human wickedness.

Critical scholarship distinguishes **two strands** woven into this narrative: a
Shemihazah strand, whose transgression is improper union, and an Asael strand,
whose transgression is improper revelation. The doubled indictment — the
Watchers condemned both for what they fathered and for what they taught — gives
the tradition its shape; the fuller analysis of the strands, the named chiefs,
and the Mesopotamian background of the material belongs to the dedicated
{% wiki(slug="watchers") %}Watchers{% end %} entry.

## Reception

The Book of the Watchers was widely read in the Second Temple period and the
first Christian centuries. The New Testament Epistle of Jude quotes 1 Enoch 1:9
directly and by name — "Enoch also, the seventh from Adam, prophesied of these"
(Jude 14–15) — and Jude 6 and 2 Peter 2:4 compress the binding of the Watchers
into a phrase. Justin Martyr, Athenagoras, Irenaeus, and Tertullian all treated
the descent of the angels as scripture; Tertullian defended the book's authority
explicitly. The Aramaic manuscripts and the Book of Giants at Qumran show that
the material was in active use there.

Its fortunes then reversed. From roughly the fourth century CE the older reading
of Genesis 6 — the *benei ha-Elohim* as heavenly beings — was displaced on both
sides: in Western Christianity by the Sethite interpretation that Augustine
consolidated, and in rabbinic Judaism by the "sons of the nobles" reading and a
recorded curse on those who translated the phrase literally. 1 Enoch fell out of
circulation everywhere except the Ethiopian canon, where it never lost its
scriptural standing. Annette Yoshiko Reed's study reconstructs this arc — the
rise, suppression, and modern recovery of the Enochic reading — and it is one of
the corpus's standing examples that the boundaries of the "canonical" record are
themselves datable editorial events rather than fixed facts.

## In the Wheel of Heaven framework

On the corpus's reading, stated in the direct voice the project assigns to
framework-internal claims: the Book of the Watchers is the Second Temple Jewish
memory of the **exiled creator faction** of the {% wiki(slug="antediluvian") %}antediluvian{% end %}
period. The Watchers are the {% wiki(slug="lucifer") %}Lucifer{% end %} faction
of the {% wiki(slug="elohim") %}Elohim{% end %} — the dissident scientists
permanently exiled on Earth after the {% wiki(slug="eden") %}Eden{% end %}
settlement, who continued to teach the Eden lineage across the age. The Nephilim
are their hybrid offspring with women of that lineage. The forbidden arts are
**technology transfer** — the sciences the home world had prohibited, taught in
defiance of the containment policy — and the text's own legal theory, that the
teachers bear responsibility for the uses of the teaching, preserves the
prosecuting authority's case against the faction. The Flood is the home-world
{% wiki(slug="theomachy") %}Council{% end %}'s response: the {% wiki(slug="great-flood") %}reset{% end %}
that ended the antediluvian world.

The two scholarly strands map onto two remembered activities of one historical
group — the faction both taught and fathered — which is why a compiled memory of
that group would carry both charges, imperfectly harmonized. The perspective of
the text is the winning side's: the teaching is a crime and the teachers are
criminals, where the corpus reads the same knowledge as the reason the Eden
lineage's trajectory was what it was. This is why the corpus values the book so
highly and reads it against its own grain — the same {% wiki(slug="doubled-signature") %}doubled
preservation{% end %} by which one episode survives in two national records with
opposite valences, the {% wiki(slug="serpent") %}Eden disclosure{% end %} and the
antediluvian teaching belonging, on this account, to one continuous story.

The claim is **framework**, not established history. Mainstream scholarship reads
1 Enoch as literature with literary sources — Enochic invention, inverted
Mesopotamian sage-lore, and priestly polemic — with no historical referent of
the kind described here, and the corpus's reading depends on the Raëlian source
material's account of the exiled faction rather than on anything the Enochic
text could establish alone. What 1 Enoch supplies is not proof but detail: where
Genesis gives four verses, the Book of the Watchers gives the group's size, its
leadership, its oath, its curriculum, and the sequence of the intervention.

## 2 Enoch and 3 Enoch

Two later, independent works travel under Enoch's name and should not be confused
with 1 Enoch. **2 Enoch** (the *Secrets of Enoch*, or Slavonic Enoch) survives
principally in Old Church Slavonic and narrates Enoch's ascent through the seven
heavens; it is here that the Watchers reappear under their Greek-derived name,
the **Grigori**, imprisoned and grieving in the fifth heaven. **3 Enoch** (the
*Sefer Hekhalot*, or Hebrew Book of Enoch) is later still, a work of Jewish
mysticism in which the patriarch is taken up and transfigured into the great
angel **Metatron**, "the lesser YHWH." It belongs not to the apocalyptic
tradition of 1 Enoch but to the {% wiki(slug="hekhalot-literature") %}Hekhalot
literature{% end %}, the pre-Kabbalistic corpus of heavenly-ascent texts, and is
treated there. The three books share a patriarch and little else; when the
corpus speaks of "the Book of Enoch" without qualification, it means 1 Enoch,
and above all its Book of the Watchers.
