**Zecharia Sitchin** (1920–2010) was an Azerbaijani-born American author who,
across the roughly thirteen volumes of his *Earth Chronicles* — beginning with
the international best-seller *The 12th Planet* (1976) — advanced the single most
detailed and widely read version of the claim that humanity was manufactured by
visitors from another world. On his reading the Sumerian
{% wiki(slug="anunnaki") %}Anunnaki{% end %} were flesh-and-blood astronauts from
a planet he called **Nibiru**, and the gods of every ancient culture are the
mythologized memory of them. For the Wheel of Heaven corpus Sitchin matters for
one blunt reason: more than any other writer, he put the Anunnaki question in
front of a mass audience — and he is also the figure from whom the corpus most
carefully distinguishes itself. It shares the broad direction of his work while
rejecting nearly all of its machinery.

The distinction is easy to blur, because the conclusion sounds similar even where
the route to it is not. Sitchin argued that the biblical and Mesopotamian "gods"
were embodied beings; so does the corpus. But the corpus reaches that reading
through the plain sense of the texts and through the Raëlian *Message*, not
through a hidden planet, a gold-mining expedition, and a chronology of DNA. Where
the two agree, they agree at the level of *identity* — that the ancient records
remember real makers — and part company almost everywhere below it.

## The Earth Chronicles

Sitchin was born in 1920 in Baku, then part of Soviet Azerbaijan, and was raised
in Mandatory Palestine, where he learned modern and biblical Hebrew. He took a
degree in economic history from the University of London and worked for years as
a journalist and editor in Israel before settling in New York. He was not a
trained Assyriologist, and this is not a small point: he taught himself to read
the cuneiform scripts on which his whole system rests, and the specialists who
read those languages professionally do not accept his renderings. His authority
was that of a gifted, self-directed amateur who had spent decades with the
Sumerian and Akkadian material.

The *Earth Chronicles* ran from ***The 12th Planet*** (1976) through *The
Stairway to Heaven* (1980), *The Wars of Gods and Men* (1985), *The Lost Realms*
(1990), *When Time Began* (1993), *The Cosmic Code* (1998), and *The End of Days*
(2007), with companion volumes such as *Genesis Revisited* (1990) and *Divine
Encounters* (1995) elaborating the same framework. The books sold in the millions
and were translated into dozens of languages; through them, and through the
television and popular-culture ecosystem that grew up around them, the word
"Anunnaki" entered ordinary vocabulary. Whatever a specialist makes of the
argument, its reach is a fact: for a very large number of readers, Sitchin *is*
the ancient-astronaut question.

## The thesis

Sitchin's reconstruction is a single, tightly interlocking system. Its keystone is
**Nibiru** — from a Sumerian number-word he associated with the *sar*, 3,600 — a
large planet he placed within our own solar system on a highly elliptical orbit
that returns to the inner system once every 3,600 years. Nibiru is the home of
the Anunnaki, and the long orbit sets the clock for everything that follows.

On Sitchin's account the Anunnaki came to Earth some 450,000 years ago to mine
**gold**, which they needed — dispersed as a fine particulate — to repair their
own planet's failing atmosphere. The lower ranks of the workforce, the Igigi,
rebelled at the labor in the mines, and so the Anunnaki solved their labor problem
by *making* a worker: the wise god Enki and a mother goddess engineered a primitive
being by splicing Anunnaki genes into *Homo erectus*, producing the first human,
whom Sitchin reads behind the biblical Adam. From there he read the Hebrew
*Nefilim* as "those who came down," identified them with the Anunnaki, and treated
essentially every pantheon — Sumerian, Egyptian, Greek, Vedic, Mesoamerican — as
the same beings under different names. Gold, orbit, genetic workforce, and a
single global cast: the pieces lock together into a vivid, self-consistent story,
and that coherence is a large part of why it is the reason most readers arrive at
the Anunnaki–Elohim question in the first place.

## The mainstream rejection

The system is rejected by the scholars best placed to test it, and the objection
deserves its own voice. The core problem is philological: Sitchin's translations
of the cuneiform are not accepted across Assyriology. Specialists who read
Sumerian and Akkadian do not find his readings in the tablets, and where his
argument depends on a particular rendering of a sign or a name, the reading that
carries the weight is usually his own rather than the field's.

The Nibiru claim is the clearest case. In the cuneiform sources *nibiru* is an
ordinary Akkadian word for a **crossing** or crossing-point, and in astronomical
texts it names a **marker-star** — a celestial reference point associated, most
prominently in *Enūma eliš*, with Jupiter as the star of Marduk — not a hidden
planet on a long orbit. The biblical scholar and Semitic-languages specialist
Michael S. Heiser assembled the cuneiform attestations in detail and concluded
that nothing in them supports a twelfth planet; the term does astronomical
bookkeeping, not planetary discovery. The **3,600-year orbit** itself is Sitchin's
own construction, read out of a number-word rather than out of any orbital
observation, and astronomy recognizes no such body in the solar system. Ronald H.
Fritze's *Invented Knowledge* (2009) places the *Earth Chronicles* squarely among
the modern pseudo-histories — inventive, internally consistent, and unsupported by
the evidence it claims to rest on. The corpus records this rejection at full
strength rather than paraphrasing it away; the popular case for the Anunnaki does
not need Sitchin's apparatus, and the parts of it that scholarship has actually
falsified should be let go.

## In the Wheel of Heaven framework

The corpus's position is best stated as a division. It keeps Sitchin's
**direction** and discards his **apparatus**. The direction is the identity claim
at the collective level: that the biblical and Mesopotamian gods remember the same
physically embodied makers, and that "god," here, is a pre-scientific culture's
word for a technologically advanced civilization it could not otherwise describe —
the reading method the corpus calls
{% wiki(slug="neo-euhemerism") %}neo-euhemerism{% end %} and the wider field calls
the {% wiki(slug="ancient-astronaut-hypothesis") %}ancient-astronaut hypothesis{% end %}.
On that much, Sitchin and the corpus agree.

The apparatus is everything the corpus sets aside: the planet Nibiru, the
3,600-year orbit, the gold-mining motive, the *Homo erectus* DNA chronology, and
the blanket merger of every pantheon into a single Sumerian roster. None of it is
load-bearing for the corpus, and most of it is what mainstream scholarship has
specifically rejected. Wheel of Heaven reads the
{% wiki(slug="anunnaki") %}Anunnaki{% end %} through the
{% wiki(slug="elohim") %}Elohim{% end %} and the *Message* frame — the same
embodied creator-civilization remembered under a Mesopotamian name — rather than
through Nibiru, and it treats the relationship between the Elohim and the various
ancient pantheons as a matter for case-by-case comparison, not automatic identity.
The antediluvian frame Sitchin folds into his chronology is read instead through
the {% wiki(slug="sumerian-king-list") %}Sumerian King List{% end %} on its own
terms. Collective identity does not license merging every named deity into one
individual: the Anunnaki and the Elohim can name the same civilization while each
proposed mapping between a Sumerian and an Egyptian or Vedic figure remains a
comparative claim to be argued one by one.

The distinction matters most in choosing whom the corpus stands with. Sitchin is
the on-ramp — the writer who made the question unignorable — but he is not the
method. The corpus's own antecedents lie in the text-led philological line: **Jean
Sendy**, who reached the embodied-makers reading of the Hebrew Bible in the 1960s
by taking the text at its plain word, as Schliemann took Homer; and **Mauro
Biglino**, who reaches substantially the same reading through a strict-literal
translation of the Hebrew. That line builds no astronomical system and needs no
hidden planet; it stays on the words on the page. It is also, tellingly,
independent of Sitchin — the philological writers the corpus most values, Biglino
and Paul Anthony Wallis among them, do not cite him at all, and their convergence
with the framework is worth more precisely because it was not routed through his
machinery. Sitchin drew the crowd to the door; the corpus reads the Anunnaki with
different tools once inside. The full identity claim, together with the boundary
against Sitchin's system, is set out in [*Are the Anunnaki the
Elohim?*](/articles/anunnaki-and-the-elohim/).
