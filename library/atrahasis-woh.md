*Atra-ḫasīs* is the Old Babylonian epic of creation and flood. It opens on the line *inūma ilū awīlum*, "When the gods, like man, bore the labor": the lesser gods, the Igigi, dig the Tigris, the Euphrates and the Apsû for forty years until they burn their tools and rise in the night against Enlil.

To relieve the gods of their toil, Enki has one god slaughtered, Wê-ila, "the god who had *ṭēmu*" (intelligence), and the birth-goddess Nintu mixes his flesh and blood into clay. Fourteen pieces are pinched off, seven male and seven female, and humankind is made to carry the *dullu*, the labor of the gods. Two puns bind the creature to its divine ingredient: the god's *ṭēmu* becomes the *eṭemmu* (spirit) that remains in humans, and Wê-ila is heard in *awīlu*, "man". Tablet II tells how a too-numerous humanity disturbs the gods, who try to thin it by plague, drought and famine. Tablet III tells of the Flood, the gods' hunger when their offerings stop, and the new order of fixed death and limits on human fertility.

The translation lets the epic speak in its own terms. Its relation to the dust and breath of Genesis 2:7, and to the wider Wheel of Heaven reading, is developed in the encyclopedia and the Explainers. Lines with a note end in a bracketed marker `[ ]`; select it to open the note.
