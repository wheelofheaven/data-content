The Baal Cycle is a Late Bronze Age myth from Ugarit (Ras Shamra, on the Syrian coast), written on clay tablets in the Ugaritic alphabetic cuneiform and found in the royal palace in 1929–1931. It was probably copied in the 14th–13th centuries BCE by the scribe Ilimilku. It tells how the storm-god Baʿlu fights Yammu (Sea) and Môtu (Death) and becomes king among the gods. No other text sheds as much light on the Northwest-Semitic background of the Hebrew Bible: YHWH's battle with the sea (Psalm 74:13–14, Psalm 89:9–10, Isaiah 27:1), El and the divine council, the *bn ʾilm* (Hebrew *bnei elohim*), Asherah, Leviathan (Ugaritic Lôtanu), and the storm imagery of Psalm 29.

**Chapter 1 (KTU 1.1–1.2)** covers the conflict with Sea: Yammu's demand for tribute, the duel, and Baʿlu's victory with two named weapons forged by the craftsman-god Kothar-wa-Khasis. Much of these tablets is broken. At KTU 1.1 IV 14 the line *šm bny yw* has been read as an early form of the name YHWH; the notes give three scholarly readings (de Moor, Cross, Smith) without choosing between them. **Chapter 2 (KTU 1.3–1.4)** covers ʿAnat's battle, ʾAṯirat's plea to ʾIlu on Baʿlu's behalf, and Kothar's building of Baʿlu's palace on Mount Ṣapunu, including the dispute over whether the palace should have a window and Baʿlu's thundering voice, which the notes compare with the seven-fold "voice of YHWH" in Psalm 29.
