Tablet XI of the Epic of Gilgamesh holds the Mesopotamian flood story best known to modern readers. Gilgamesh, looking for a way to escape death, reaches Utnapishtim, the one man the gods made immortal. Utnapishtim tells him how the gods resolved to flood the earth; how Ea warned him through a reed wall; how he built a boat of seven decks and loaded his family, craftsmen and "the seed of all living creatures"; how the storm raged for six days and seven nights; how the boat grounded on Mount Nimush and he sent out a dove, a swallow and a raven; and how, after his sacrifice, the gods "gathered like flies" and he and his wife were made "like the gods". Gilgamesh then fails a test of sleep, and a serpent steals the plant of rejuvenation from him on his way home to Uruk.

The *Yes... I am Raelian* commentary names the Epic of Gilgamesh among the texts "closer to the truth". The text survives on tablets from the library of Ashurbanipal at Nineveh (7th century BCE), and its publication by George Smith in 1872–1873 first showed a Mesopotamian forerunner of the biblical flood. The notes follow the parallels line by line: Genesis 6–9, the Sumerian *Flood Story*, *Atra-ḫasīs* (whose hero is named at line 189 as another name for Utnapishtim), Berossus and Qur'an 11:25–49. They also compare Utnapishtim's immortality with Adapa's loss of it. Lines with a note end in a bracketed marker `[ ]`; select it to open the note.
